## What you'll learn

- Convert a nines target into the minutes of failure it actually permits.
- Compose availability across dependencies and across redundant copies.
- Separate durability from availability, and say what each mechanism buys.
- Describe reliability in terms of failure rate, recovery time and blast radius.

## Intuition

**Availability** is the fraction of requests a system answers successfully over
a stated window. Note what that definition does not say: it says nothing about
whether a process was running. A service whose machines are all up and whose
every response is a 500 has an availability of zero, and a service that answers
with slightly stale data is available.

**Durability** is the probability that data, once written and acknowledged, is
still there and still correct later. The two properties are independent.
Availability is a promise about _now_: can you get an answer? Durability is a
promise about _ever_: is the thing you stored last year still stored? A database
that has lost its network is unavailable and perfectly durable, and that is
usually the right way for a database to fail.

**Nines are an error budget.** "99.9% available" reads like an aspiration and
behaves like an allowance: it says you may fail for roughly forty-four minutes
a month, and that this is expected rather than shameful. Framing it that way is
the whole point, because it turns an argument about whether to ship into a
question about how much budget is left.

## How it works

Start by converting the target into time, since a percentage is hard to reason
about and minutes are not:

```text
one year = 365 × 24 = 8,760 hours; one month ≈ 730 hours

99%      allows 1%     of 730 h ≈ 7.3 hours   per month
99.9%    allows 0.1%   of 730 h ≈ 44 minutes  per month
99.99%   allows 0.01%  of 730 h ≈ 4.4 minutes per month
99.999%  allows 0.001% of 730 h ≈ 26 seconds  per month
```

Read the last line carefully. Twenty-six seconds a month is less than one
careless deploy, so five nines is a claim about automation rather than effort:
nobody can be paged, diagnose and fix inside that budget.

Availability also composes badly. If a request must touch five services in
sequence and all five must succeed, their availabilities multiply:

```text
five dependencies in series, each 99.9% available
0.999 ^ 5 ≈ 0.995 → 99.5%, or roughly 3.6 hours of failure per month
```

Five well-run services produced a badly-run system. The fix is not a further
nine from each dependency — the expensive direction — but removing the
seriality: make the call optional and degrade without it, serve a cached answer,
or move the work off the request path.

Redundancy composes in the opposite direction, and this is where the arithmetic
lies to you:

```text
two replicas, each unavailable 1% of the time
both down at once, IF the failures are independent = 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001
→ 99.99% available
```

Four nines from two ordinary machines, on paper. In practice the replicas share
a rack, a network, a configuration push, a certificate expiry and a bug, and
every one of those is a way to fail at the same moment. **Correlated failure is
why measured availability never reaches the calculated figure**, and it is why
"spread across failure domains" is an instruction rather than a slogan: the
arithmetic is only as good as the independence.

A second decomposition points at the cheaper lever:

```text
availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)
  mean time between failures 30 days = 43,200 minutes

recovery in 60 minutes → 43,200 ÷ 43,260 ≈ 99.86%
recovery in  5 minutes → 43,200 ÷ 43,205 ≈ 99.988%
```

The failure rate did not change; only the recovery time did, and downtime fell
twelvefold — about a nine. Note that 99.988% is a little short of 99.99%: five
minutes of recovery a month is 5.1 minutes of downtime against a 4.4-minute
budget, so the target needs recovery inside four minutes, not five. That is the
kind of gap a table of nines makes visible and an intuition does not. Making failures rarer is research; making recovery faster is
engineering — rollbacks that work, health checks that eject a bad instance,
runbooks, and a deploy that can be undone in one step.

Durability is bought differently, with four mechanisms:

- **Acknowledge late.** A write confirmed from one machine's memory is not
  durable; a write confirmed after it is on stable storage in more than one
  failure domain is. That is the largest durability decision in most systems,
  and it costs a round trip on every write.
- **Keep redundant copies.** Replicas across failure domains, or erasure coding,
  which trades processor time for far less storage at the same redundancy.
- **Keep copies in the past.** Replication propagates a bad delete or a corrupt
  row at the speed of the network. A backup is a copy of an earlier moment, and
  the only defence against a logical error.
- **Verify.** Checksums that are actually read, restores that are actually
  practised. An unrestored backup is an assumption.

<Callout variant="note">
  **Reading a durability figure.** Object storage is commonly specified at
  around eleven nines of annual durability — generally accepted industry
  framing, not a claim about any one provider. The figure describes expected
  object loss from hardware and media failure under the service's own
  replication scheme. It says nothing about your application deleting the wrong
  key, an encryption key being lost, or a migration overwriting a column — which
  are the ways data usually disappears.
</Callout>

Reliability is the umbrella over both: a reliable system does the right thing,
when asked, over time. Availability and durability are two measurable facets of
it, and correctness is a third. Reliability work reduces exactly three
quantities — how often failures happen, how long recovery takes, and how much of
the system one failure takes with it.

## Trade-offs

**Each nine costs roughly an order of magnitude more than the last.** Going from
99.9% to 99.99% typically means a second region, tested automatic failover and a
staffed rotation. Ask what an hour of downtime costs before buying it; for a
great many systems 99.9% is the honest answer.

**Durability fights write latency.** Acknowledging a write only after a second
copy has it is what makes it durable, and the more distant that copy, the slower
every write becomes — the same trade the
[latency budget](/learn/sd-latency-and-throughput/) prices, seen from the other
side.

**Degrading beats failing, and is often cheaper than a nine.** A page that
renders an unranked list when the ranking service is down is _available_ by the
definition that matters, and that is a smaller change than making the ranking
service highly available. Every optional dependency deserves a stated behaviour
for when it is absent.

## Common pitfalls

- **Reporting process uptime instead of request success.** Tempting because
  uptime is trivial to measure and always looks good; it misses every failure
  where the service answers and the answer is wrong.
- **Quoting nines with no window and no scope.** 99.99% over a year and 99.99%
  over a month permit very different worst days, and "the API" is not a scope if
  half its endpoints are cached and half are not.
- **Treating replicas as backups.** Tempting because both are extra copies. A
  replica defends against a machine dying; a backup defends against a command
  you ran.
- **Setting the internal target equal to the contractual one.** The SLA is a
  promise with a remedy attached; the SLO should be tighter, so that missing it
  is a signal rather than an invoice.

## Interview notes

When asked how available something must be, answer with the cost of failure
rather than a number pulled from the air: what a minute of downtime costs, who
notices, and whether a retry a minute later is acceptable. Then state the target
as a budget — "99.9%, so about forty-four minutes a month, spent on deploys" —
because that is the version an operations team can work with.

Then say which of the two promises each part makes. In a Netflix-like platform,
"playback should not stall" is availability and "a purchase record must never be
lost" is durability, and they lead to different designs: caching and degradation
for the first, acknowledged multi-copy writes and backups for the second.

## Key takeaways

- Availability is **successful requests over a window**, not process uptime, and
  99.9% is about 44 minutes of failure per month.
- **Serial dependencies multiply availability down**: five 99.9% services in a
  row give 99.5%. Remove the seriality rather than buying nines.
- **Redundancy only helps to the extent failures are independent**, and shared
  racks, configuration and code make them correlated.
- Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR), so **cutting recovery time is usually the
  cheapest nine** available.
- Durability is a separate promise, kept by acknowledging writes late, spreading
  copies across failure domains, and keeping backups — which replication is not.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A request path calls five services in sequence, each with a measured availability of 99.9%. What is the path's availability, and what is the cheapest way to improve it?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  All five must succeed, so the availabilities multiply: 0.999 to the fifth
  power is about 0.995, or 99.5% — roughly 3.6 hours of failure a month, seven
  times the budget of any single dependency. The cheapest improvement is almost
  never pushing each service to 99.99%, which is an order of magnitude of work
  five times over. It is breaking the chain: make two calls optional with a
  defined degraded response, serve one from a cache that tolerates staleness,
  and move one off the request path. A dependency you can succeed without has
  stopped multiplying.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A team wants to move from 99.9% to 99.99% and proposes buying more reliable hardware. What would you suggest they measure first, and why?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Measure the mean time to recovery, because availability is MTBF ÷ (MTBF +
  MTTR) and MTTR is the term they can change quickly. With a failure every 30
  days, an hour of recovery gives about 99.86%; the same failure rate recovered
  in four minutes gives about 99.99% — the target, with no hardware bought.
  Better hardware raises MTBF, which is slower, costlier, and does nothing for
  the failures that come from configuration and code rather than from metal. The
  practical work is a one-step rollback, health checks that eject a bad instance
  automatically, and an alert that fires before a user files a report.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. "We keep three synchronous replicas of the database, so we do not need backups." What is wrong with this, and what class of failure is each mechanism for?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Replication copies whatever the primary does, including the wrong things. A
  mistaken DELETE, a bad migration, an application bug writing corrupt rows or a
  compromised credential reaches all three replicas within milliseconds — three
  copies of the damage. Replication defends against a *machine* failing: it
  keeps data available when a node or a zone is gone. A backup is a copy of an
  earlier moment, and the only defence against a *logical* failure, because it
  is the only copy that does not contain the mistake. A backup nobody has
  restored is a belief rather than a mechanism.
</PracticeCheck>
