## What you'll learn

- Trace a recommendation from a listening event through features, candidates and ranking.
- Split the pipeline into the part that runs hourly and the part that runs in 50 ms.
- Name the feedback loops the design creates, and what limits them.

## Intuition

**A recommender is a funnel with two very different cost budgets.** A hundred
million tracks cannot all be scored for one listener opening one screen: at even
a microsecond each that is a hundred seconds of work for a page that has about
fifty milliseconds. So the work is split. A **candidate generation** stage
narrows a hundred million to a few thousand using cheap lookups that were mostly
precomputed. A **ranking** stage scores those few thousand with an expensive
model that can afford many features per item, and returns the thirty a listener
sees.

That two-stage split is **publicly documented**: Covington, Adams and Sargin
describe exactly this architecture — candidate generation followed by ranking —
for YouTube's own production recommender in _Deep Neural Networks for YouTube
Recommendations_ (RecSys, 2016), including the observation that the ranking stage
was trained to predict expected watch time rather than clicks. The same funnel is
generally accepted industry architecture for any recommender over a large
catalogue, and the Spotify-like pipeline below differs from it only in the
signals it consumes.

The second model: **the pipeline is one loop, not one direction.** Recommendations
produce plays, plays produce events, events produce the features that produce the
next recommendations. Every property of the system, good and bad, comes from that
loop closing.

## How it works

Five stages. The first three run offline on the asynchronous path; the last two
run inside a request.

**1. Event collection.** The listening events from the previous lessons — 50,000
per second — are the raw material. Two kinds matter. _Explicit_ signals are rare
and unambiguous: a like, a save to a playlist, a follow. _Implicit_ signals are
plentiful and noisy: a full play, a skip in the first thirty seconds, a repeat,
the hour of day. **The skip is the cheapest strong negative signal in music**,
and few products have an equivalent: it is a negative judgement, it arrives
within seconds, and listeners produce them constantly without being asked.

**2. Feature generation.** Batch jobs read the log and build three groups of
features: per listener (recent artists, genre mix, time-of-day profile), per
track (play counts, skip rate, co-occurrence with other tracks in playlists and
sessions, and audio-derived attributes such as tempo and energy), and per
context (device, hour, what is playing now). Most of this is hourly or daily
work. A thin streaming path keeps a short window of very recent behaviour fresh,
because "you played this five minutes ago" is a feature a daily batch cannot
provide.

**3. Candidate generation.** Several cheap sources run in parallel and their
results are unioned:

| Source                   | How it works                               | Answers                         |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Collaborative            | Listeners who played this also played that | Popular pairings                |
| Playlist co-occurrence   | Tracks that sit together in many playlists | Human curation at scale         |
| Content similarity       | Nearest neighbours in audio-feature space  | Cold-start tracks with no plays |
| Editorial and popularity | Curated lists, charts per market           | New listeners, new releases     |
| Session continuation     | More of what is playing right now          | The next track in a radio queue |

Each source returns a few hundred items from a precomputed index, so the stage
costs a handful of key lookups rather than a model evaluation. The union is a few
thousand candidates, deliberately over-inclusive — anything not in it can never
be recommended, so recall matters here and precision does not.

**4. Ranking.** One model scores the candidate set with far more features per
item than candidate generation could afford, and sorts it. Then the rules that
are not the model's business are applied: filter anything not licensed in this
territory, drop what the listener already heard today, and enforce diversity so
five tracks by one artist do not fill a shelf. Keeping those as explicit
post-filters rather than model features is what makes them auditable when
somebody asks why a track appeared.

**5. Feedback.** The listener plays, skips or saves, and those events re-enter
stage 1.

**Offline against online** is the axis that decides where each stage lives:

```text
Offline (hourly to daily)     Online (inside 50 ms)
  feature aggregation           read precomputed candidates
  co-occurrence indexes         apply recent-session features
  embedding training            score with the ranking model
  precomputed home shelves      filter rights, dedupe, diversify
  model training and eval       log what was shown, for training
```

The rule of thumb: **anything that depends on all listeners is offline; anything
that depends on this listener in this moment is online.** Precomputing a whole
home screen per listener is a third option worth knowing — it makes the request
almost free and costs a job that runs for fifty million listeners whether they
open the app or not, so it suits shelves that change daily and not a radio queue
that reacts to a skip.

<Callout variant="note">
  **Log what was shown, not only what was played.** A model trained only on
  plays learns from a sample the previous model chose, and can never learn that
  something it never showed would have worked. Recording impressions alongside
  outcomes is what makes the training data honest, and it is far cheaper to add
  now than to backfill later.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**When not to build this at all.** A new product with no listening history has
nothing to learn from, and editorial lists plus per-market popularity beat a
model trained on a thousand events. Personalisation earns its complexity once
there is enough behaviour per listener to distinguish them; before that it is a
pipeline with an opinion about noise.

**Precomputed against live.** Precomputing is cheap to serve, survives a partial
outage — a stale shelf is still a shelf — and cannot react to what happened a
minute ago. Live ranking reacts and puts a model on the critical path of a screen
that has a latency budget. Most platforms run both: precomputed shelves for
browse, live ranking for the queue.

**The feedback loop is also a failure mode.** Recommending popular tracks
produces plays for popular tracks, which raises their popularity features.
Without deliberate exploration — reserving a small share of slots for items the
model is unsure about — the catalogue collapses toward a head that gets narrower
every week, and a hundred million tracks becomes a very expensive way to serve
ten thousand.

**Offline metrics do not settle anything.** A model that scores better on
held-out data can still perform worse with listeners, because the held-out data
was collected under the old model. The decision comes from a controlled
experiment on live traffic, which means recommendation work is also
experimentation infrastructure work.

## Common pitfalls

- **Optimising the wrong objective.** Clicks or starts are trivially maximised by
  attractive titles; a play-to-completion or a save is a much harder thing to
  fake, which is exactly why it is a better target.
- **Cold start treated as an edge case.** Every new release and every new listener
  passes through it daily, and the answers — content features for tracks,
  onboarding picks and popularity for listeners — are architecture, not patches.
- **Filtering rights in the model.** A licensing rule expressed as a feature is a
  suggestion. Expressed as a post-filter it is a guarantee.
- **One giant model in the request path.** It scores fewer items per millisecond
  than the funnel does, so it either times out or forces a candidate set so small
  that the interesting recommendations were discarded before ranking.

## Interview notes

Lead with the signal rather than the funnel. "The skip is the cheapest strong
negative signal in music — a negative judgement that arrives within seconds, and
listeners produce them constantly without being asked — and few products have an
equivalent" says something about this catalogue a generic pipeline answer never
reaches.

Then earn the two-stage split with the arithmetic instead of asserting it: a
hundred million tracks at even a microsecond each is a hundred seconds of work
for a page that has about fifty milliseconds. That sentence produces the funnel;
"we'd use collaborative filtering" names a technique and settles nothing.

## Key takeaways

- Recommendation is a **two-stage funnel**: cheap candidate generation over the
  whole catalogue, expensive ranking over a few thousand items.
- **Candidate generation optimises recall**; anything it omits is unreachable no
  matter how good the ranker is.
- Features that depend on **all listeners are computed offline**; features that
  depend on **this listener now** are applied online.
- **Skips are the cheapest strong negative signal** in music, and cold start is a
  daily event rather than an exception.
- The loop from recommendation to play to feature **improves and narrows** the
  system at the same time; exploration and live experiments are how the narrowing
  is kept in check.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. Why not skip candidate generation and let one strong model score the whole catalogue, given enough serving hardware?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Because the arithmetic does not survive the latency budget. A hundred million
  items at even a microsecond of model evaluation each is a hundred seconds of
  CPU for one screen that has around fifty milliseconds, so reaching the answer
  in time would need thousands of cores per request — and that is per listener,
  at thousands of requests per second. The funnel spends its budget where it
  changes the outcome: cheap precomputed lookups to reach a few thousand
  plausible items, then the expensive model only on those. The trade is real —
  anything candidate generation misses can never be ranked — which is why that
  stage is tuned for recall and uses several independent sources rather than
  one.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A new track by an unknown artist is released today. It has no plays, no playlist co-occurrence and no likes. How can it be recommended at all, and why is that not a special case?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Through the candidate sources that do not need behavioural history: content
  similarity puts it near tracks with comparable audio attributes, and editorial
  or new-release shelves place it in front of listeners deliberately. Both
  produce the first plays, and those plays generate the collaborative signals
  everything else needs — so cold start is the on-ramp to the rest of the
  pipeline rather than a gap in it. It is not a special case because it happens
  continuously: every release and every new listener starts here, so a design
  that handles cold start only as a fallback is failing at its most common
  input.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A ranking model is retrained on last month's plays and scores clearly better offline than the model in production. Why is that not enough to ship it?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Because last month's plays are not a neutral sample of listener preference —
  they are what the *current* model chose to show, and what listeners then did
  with it. A new model that agrees with the old one's choices scores well on
  that data by construction, and a model that would have surfaced something
  better is penalised for disagreeing with a history where that item was never
  shown. Offline evaluation can therefore rank the safer model higher while the
  bolder one performs better with real listeners. The decision needs a
  controlled experiment on live traffic, with impressions logged so the next
  round of training sees what was shown and not only what was played.
</PracticeCheck>
