## What you'll learn

- Run a system design as an ordered sequence instead of a brainstorm.
- Say which question each step answers, and what its output feeds.
- State a design decision together with the trade-off that made it.

## Intuition

"Design a system like YouTube" is not a question. It is a topic. It has no
answer until someone decides how many users, which features, what may be slow,
and what may occasionally be wrong. Most bad designs are not wrong about
technology — they are answers to a question nobody pinned down.

**The workflow is a funnel, not a checklist.** Each step narrows what the next
one may consider. You cannot choose a database before you know the read/write
ratio, or know that before you know the features, or know those before you know
who the system is for. Out of order, you will spend the design arguing about
Kafka.

The second idea matters as much: **a design is a set of trade-offs, not a set of
choices.** "We use a cache" is not a decision. "We cache the catalogue for 60
seconds, which costs up to a minute of staleness after an edit and saves roughly
95% of catalogue reads" is. The first sentence cannot be disagreed with; the
second can, and that is what makes it engineering.

<Callout variant="note">
  **How this course labels claims.** Real systems are mostly private. Every
  architectural statement here is labelled as one of three things: *publicly
  documented* (with the source named — Netflix Open Connect, Spotify's
  engineering blog, the 2016 YouTube recommendations paper), *generally accepted
  industry architecture*, or *a teaching assumption* we are making to keep the
  example concrete. When we design a platform, we call it "Netflix-like", never
  Netflix — because we are designing a system that has to solve the same
  problems, not reproducing one we cannot see.
</Callout>

## How it works

Thirteen steps. Each one produces something the next step consumes.

| #   | Step                             | The question it answers                        | Output                                       |
| --- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Clarify requirements             | What must it do?                               | A feature list                               |
| 2   | Define scope                     | What are we _not_ building?                    | An explicit out-of-scope list                |
| 3   | Estimate scale                   | How big is this?                               | Users, QPS, storage, bandwidth               |
| 4   | Identify core entities           | What are the nouns?                            | 4–8 entities and their relations             |
| 5   | Design the API                   | How is it used?                                | Endpoints, verbs, payload shapes             |
| 6   | Choose storage                   | Where do the bytes live?                       | A store per entity, with a reason            |
| 7   | Draw the high-level architecture | How do the parts fit?                          | Boxes and arrows                             |
| 8   | Find the bottleneck              | What breaks first?                             | One named component                          |
| 9   | Add caching                      | What is read far more than written?            | Cache layer, key, TTL, invalidation          |
| 10  | Add asynchronous processing      | What can happen later?                         | Queue or stream, and its consumers           |
| 11  | Reliability                      | What happens when a part dies?                 | Replication, retries, degradation            |
| 12  | Security and privacy             | Who may see what?                              | AuthN, authZ, data handling                  |
| 13  | Observability and trade-offs     | How do we know it works, and what did it cost? | Metrics, and a stated trade-off per decision |

**Steps 1 and 2 — requirements and scope.** Separate _functional_ requirements
(upload a video, follow a channel) from _non-functional_ ones (99.9% available,
p99 under 300 ms, durable for ten years). The non-functional list is where the
architecture comes from: "playback must never stall" forces a CDN long before you
have chosen a language. Scope is the other half — writing down "no live
streaming, no monetisation" is what stops the design sprawling.

**Step 3 — estimate scale.** Order of magnitude, with the arithmetic shown. If
10 million daily users each read 20 items:

```text
10,000,000 users × 20 reads = 200,000,000 reads/day
200,000,000 ÷ 86,400 s      ≈ 2,300 reads/second average
peak ≈ 3× average           ≈ 7,000 reads/second
writes at 1% of reads       ≈ 70 writes/second
```

That single calculation has already made decisions for you. Seven thousand reads
per second is comfortably one cached read path; seventy writes per second is a
single well-indexed relational primary. Had it come out at 700,000 reads per
second, the shape of every later step would differ. **This is why step 3 comes
before step 6** — the number is what makes "PostgreSQL" or "a partitioned
key-value store" an argued answer rather than a preference.

**Steps 4 to 7 — entities, API, storage, shape.** Name the nouns and their
relationships, then write the handful of endpoints a client actually needs; the
API is where hidden requirements surface, because the moment you write
`GET /feed` you have to say what ordering means. Then pick a store per entity and
say why — this one needs transactions, that one is write-heavy and never joined,
this one is 100 KB blobs — and draw the boxes.

**Steps 8 to 10 — survive your own traffic.** Find the component that saturates
first, and only then reach for a cache or a queue. Caching answers "read far more
often than written"; asynchronous processing answers "the user does not need to
wait for this". Both add a failure mode — a stale read, a delayed job — and
naming it is part of using them.

**Steps 11 to 13 — survive reality.** What happens when a replica, a zone or a
region is gone? What does the system do when a dependency is slow rather than
down? Who may read this row? And what would tell you it is broken before a user
does?

## Trade-offs

The workflow itself has costs worth knowing.

**It is linear, and design is not.** Step 8 regularly sends you back to step 6.
That is the workflow working: a bad storage choice found at the bottleneck step
is at its cheapest. Treat the order as the order you _first_ visit things.

**It biases toward completeness over depth.** Thirteen steps in forty minutes
means none of them is deep. The value is coverage — it is hard to forget
authentication when it is line 12 — but coverage is not rigour.

**It assumes the requirements can be known.** Sometimes the honest answer at step
1 is "nobody knows yet", and the right architecture is the one cheapest to throw
away.

## Common pitfalls

- **Naming technologies at step 1.** "We'll use Kafka" before there is a write
  volume is a preference wearing a decision's clothes. The tool comes after the
  number.
- **Skipping the estimate because it feels made up.** An order of magnitude
  derived from stated assumptions is falsifiable — someone can tell you the
  assumption is wrong. No number is not.
- **Designing for a scale nobody asked for.** Partitioning a table that will hold
  fifty thousand rows is cost with no benefit.
- **Listing components instead of arguing for them.** Every box should answer
  "what breaks if I remove it?" If nothing does, remove it.
- **Leaving out the failure story.** A design that describes only the happy path
  has not been designed; it has been imagined.

## Interview notes

In an interview this workflow is also a script, and the first two steps are
graded hardest. Spending three minutes narrowing "design Twitter" to "a read-heavy
timeline for 10 million daily users, no DMs, no search" reads as seniority,
because it is what the job actually is.

Two habits carry disproportionate weight. **Say the number out loud** — the
arithmetic in step 3 shows how you think, which is the thing being assessed. And
**state the trade-off with every choice**: "I'd cache the profile for 30 seconds;
the cost is that a name change takes up to half a minute to appear everywhere,
which I think is acceptable here" is a complete answer. "I'd add Redis" is not.

## Key takeaways

- A system design is a **funnel**: requirements narrow scope, scope narrows
  scale, scale narrows storage. Out of order, the steps stop constraining each
  other.
- **Non-functional requirements produce the architecture.** Availability, latency
  and consistency targets decide more than the feature list does.
- **Estimate before you choose.** The order of magnitude turns a technology
  preference into an argued decision.
- Every decision ships with **the trade-off that made it**. A choice you cannot
  argue against has not been justified.
- Going back a step is the workflow succeeding, not failing.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. Why does estimating scale (step 3) come before choosing storage (step 6) rather than after?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Because the estimate is what makes the storage choice arguable. At 70 writes
  per second a single relational primary with good indexes is plainly enough,
  and partitioning would be cost with no benefit; at 70,000 writes per second
  the same choice is obviously wrong and the design needs partitioning, a
  different consistency model, or both. Choose first and the number can only
  ever confirm or embarrass you — and in practice people quietly discard the
  number.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A design says "we add a cache in front of the database". What is missing before that counts as a decision?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Four things: *what* is cached (which read path, and its key), *why* it is a
  candidate (the read-to-write ratio that makes it worth it), *how long* it may
  be stale (the TTL, or the invalidation event), and *what breaks* when the
  cache is empty or gone — if the database cannot survive a cold cache, the
  cache is not an optimisation, it is a load-bearing component with no
  replication. A cache always trades freshness for load; a decision names both
  sides.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. At step 8 you find the bottleneck is the database's write path, and you realise at step 6 you chose the wrong store. Have you used the workflow wrongly?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  No — that is the workflow doing its job. The steps are the order you first
  visit the questions, not a promise never to return. Finding a storage mistake
  at the bottleneck step is the cheapest possible moment to find it: nothing has
  been built, and you now have the one thing step 6 lacked, which is a concrete
  reason. What *would* be using it wrongly is noticing the problem and carrying
  on to step 9 to cache around it, because a cache in front of a write
  bottleneck fixes nothing.
</PracticeCheck>
