## What you'll learn

- Explain what the claim/volume split buys, and which side each role owns.
- Read a claim's phase and a volume's, and say what each one means.
- Choose a reclaim policy deliberately, and recognise a `Released` volume.
- Diagnose a claim that will not leave `Pending`.

## Intuition

The previous lesson ended at the boundary: `emptyDir` has the Pod's lifetime, and
some data has to outlive every Pod that touches it. Kubernetes solves that with
two objects rather than one, and the reason is worth understanding before the
mechanics.

**A PersistentVolumeClaim is a purchase order; a PersistentVolume is what
arrives.** The person writing the workload states requirements — this much space,
this access mode, this class of storage — and never names a disk, a device path
or a cloud volume id. Somebody else supplies: an administrator who created
volumes in advance, or a provisioner that makes one on demand.

Two things follow from the split. The first is **portability**: the same
Deployment applies unchanged on a laptop cluster, a managed cloud cluster and a
bare-metal one, because nothing in it is specific to any of them. The second is
**identity for the data**. A claim is a namespaced object with a name, so the
data has a name too — one that outlives every Pod that ever mounted it, and one
you can look at with `kubectl get pvc` long after the workload is gone.

## How it works

The claim is the object you write, and it is short:

```yaml
apiVersion: v1
kind: PersistentVolumeClaim
metadata:
  name: uploads
spec:
  accessModes:
    - ReadWriteOnce
  resources:
    requests:
      storage: 10Gi
```

A Pod then mounts it exactly like any other volume — the `persistentVolumeClaim`
source replaces `emptyDir`, and nothing else about the container changes:

```yaml
apiVersion: v1
kind: Pod
metadata:
  name: web
spec:
  containers:
    - name: app
      image: nginx:1.29
      volumeMounts:
        - name: uploads
          mountPath: /srv/uploads
  volumes:
    - name: uploads
      persistentVolumeClaim:
        claimName: uploads
```

A control loop inside the control plane does the matching. It looks for a
PersistentVolume that is at least as large as the request, offers the access
modes asked for, and carries the same `storageClassName`. Binding is **exclusive
and one-to-one**: once a claim and a volume are bound, neither is available to
anything else, and a 100Gi volume bound to a 10Gi claim gives that claim all
100Gi and wastes the rest.

Where does the volume come from? Usually a provisioner makes it, which is the
next lesson. The other half is a volume somebody wrote by hand — a disk that
already exists, on a machine you have chosen:

```yaml
apiVersion: v1
kind: PersistentVolume
metadata:
  name: uploads-local
spec:
  capacity:
    storage: 10Gi
  accessModes:
    - ReadWriteOnce
  persistentVolumeReclaimPolicy: Retain
  storageClassName: manual
  local:
    path: /mnt/disks/uploads
  nodeAffinity:
    required:
      nodeSelectorTerms:
        - matchExpressions:
            - key: kubernetes.io/hostname
              operator: In
              values: ['worker-1']
```

A `local` volume is a disk mounted on one node, and the `nodeAffinity` block is
not optional decoration: it is what tells the scheduler that a Pod using this
volume can only run on `worker-1`. For the claim above to bind to it, the claim
must also say `storageClassName: manual` — an empty or absent class does not
match a volume that has one.

### Phases, and what they are telling you

`kubectl get pv` and `kubectl get pvc` both show a phase, and the two vocabularies
are not the same.

| Object | Phase       | Meaning                                                                        |
| ------ | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| PVC    | `Pending`   | Nothing has satisfied it yet — no matching volume, or it is waiting on purpose |
| PVC    | `Bound`     | Matched to a volume, and usable                                                |
| PV     | `Available` | Exists, matches nothing yet                                                    |
| PV     | `Bound`     | Claimed                                                                        |
| PV     | `Released`  | Its claim was deleted; the data is still here and nothing may bind to it       |
| PV     | `Failed`    | Automatic reclamation ran and did not succeed                                  |

`Released` is the phase worth memorising, because it is the one people read as a
bug. It means the claim is gone, the reclaim policy said `Retain`, and the volume
is deliberately parked: still holding the data, still refusing to bind to
anything else. It stays that way until an administrator either copies the data
off and deletes it, or clears `spec.claimRef` so it can go back to `Available`.

```bash
kubectl get pvc uploads
kubectl describe pvc uploads
kubectl patch pv uploads-local --type=merge -p '{"spec":{"claimRef":null}}'
```

### The reclaim policy decides whether the data survives

`persistentVolumeReclaimPolicy` has two values you will use:

- **`Retain`** — deleting the claim leaves the volume and its contents alone, in
  `Released`. Recovery is manual, and that is the point.
- **`Delete`** — deleting the claim deletes the PersistentVolume **and the
  underlying storage**. The disk in the cloud provider goes with it.

The defaults are not the same on both paths, and this is where data gets
destroyed accidentally. A PersistentVolume you write by hand defaults to
`Retain`. A **dynamically provisioned** one takes its policy from its
StorageClass, and that field's default is `Delete` — so on most clusters, on the
path almost everyone uses, deleting a claim destroys the data.

<Callout variant="warning">
  Deleting a Namespace deletes the PersistentVolumeClaims inside it. With the
  usual `Delete` policy that destroys the volumes too, with no second
  confirmation and no undo. "Let me clean up that old Namespace" is one of the
  more expensive sentences in Kubernetes operations.
</Callout>

Kubernetes does defend the live case. A finalizer keeps a claim that is still
mounted by a running Pod from actually going away: `kubectl delete pvc` returns,
the object sits in `Terminating`, and the deletion completes when the last Pod
using it stops. The same protection keeps a bound volume from being deleted out
from under its claim.

## Trade-offs

**Static volumes give control and cost attention.** Pre-creating volumes is the
right answer when the storage already exists, when a specific machine's NVMe
drive is the whole point, or when the data was loaded before Kubernetes ever saw
it. The cost is a human in the provisioning loop and a class of failure where a
claim waits forever because nobody made its volume.

**The claim's request is a floor, not a quota.** It tells the provisioner how
much to make and the matcher how much is enough. Nothing enforces that your
application stays inside it — you get a filesystem, and filling a filesystem is
an application problem that Kubernetes will not report as a storage event.

**The indirection costs one more thing to look at.** When a Pod is `Pending`, the
answer may be two objects away: the Pod waits on the claim, the claim waits on a
volume, and only `kubectl describe pvc` says which.

## Common pitfalls

- **Deleting a Namespace to tidy up.** It takes the claims, and the default
  reclaim policy takes the data. Check `kubectl get pvc -n <namespace>` first, and
  check the reclaim policy of what they are bound to.
- **Reading `Pending` as broken.** A claim can be pending because nothing
  matches, because no default class exists, or entirely on purpose while it waits
  for a Pod to be scheduled. The events on the claim distinguish them.
- **Expecting a `Released` volume to rebind.** It never does on its own, however
  correct the new claim looks. Clearing `claimRef` is a deliberate act, and it
  hands the old data to the new claim — which is sometimes what you want and
  sometimes a leak.
- **Removing a finalizer to force a stuck deletion.** The claim is stuck because
  something is still using it. Stripping the finalizer deletes the object while
  the mount is live, and the outcome ranges from a hung node to a corrupted
  filesystem.
- **Trusting the size on the claim.** Matching is "at least as large", so a claim
  may be bound to a much larger volume — and on a dynamic path, to exactly the
  size the driver rounds you up to.

## Key takeaways

- The claim/volume split keeps **disk identity out of workload manifests**, which
  is what makes the same manifest portable across clusters.
- Binding is **exclusive and one-to-one**, and matching is on size, access modes
  and `storageClassName`.
- A **hand-written PersistentVolume defaults to `Retain`; a StorageClass defaults
  to `Delete`** — the common path is the destructive one.
- `Released` means the data is intact and deliberately unbindable. It requires a
  human decision, not a retry.
- Deleting a claim that a Pod still mounts leaves it `Terminating` until the Pod
  stops. That is protection working, not a stuck object to force.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A claim asks for 10Gi and binds to a volume whose capacity is 100Gi. How much space does the Pod get, and what happened to the other 90Gi?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  The Pod gets the whole 100Gi filesystem, and the other 90Gi is wasted — bound
  exclusively to this claim and unavailable to anything else. Matching is "at
  least as large as the request", not "equal to it", and binding is one-to-one,
  so an oversized volume is not shared out. This is mostly a static-provisioning
  hazard: a pool of pre-created large volumes will happily satisfy small claims
  and burn the difference. It is also why the request should be read as an
  instruction to the provisioner rather than as a promise about what the
  application will see.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A team deletes a Namespace containing a database's claim. The volume was dynamically provisioned on a cloud cluster with the usual settings. What is the state of the data, and which single field would have changed the outcome?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  The data is gone, and gone at the cloud provider — not merely detached.
  Deleting the Namespace deletes the claim inside it; a dynamically provisioned
  volume inherits its reclaim policy from its StorageClass; and that field
  defaults to `Delete`, which deletes the PersistentVolume and the underlying
  disk with it. The field is `reclaimPolicy` on the StorageClass (or
  `persistentVolumeReclaimPolicy` patched onto the volume itself). Set to
  `Retain`, the same deletion would have left the volume in `Released` with the
  data intact, recoverable by clearing its `claimRef`. This is exactly why a
  snapshot or backup is not optional for data you care about — the next lessons
  return to it.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. `kubectl get pvc` shows `Terminating` for a claim you deleted five minutes ago. A colleague suggests editing the object to remove its finalizer. What is actually happening, and why is that the wrong move?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  The claim is held by the `kubernetes.io/pvc-protection` finalizer because at
  least one running Pod still mounts it. The deletion is real and queued; it
  completes as soon as the last such Pod stops, so the fix is to find that Pod
  and remove it — scale the workload down, or let the rollout finish. Removing
  the finalizer deletes the API object while the volume is still mounted and
  being written to, which detaches storage from underneath a live process: at
  best a hung mount on the node, at worst a corrupted filesystem, and in either
  case the claim is gone while the volume is left in an inconsistent state. The
  finalizer is the protection, not the problem.
</PracticeCheck>
