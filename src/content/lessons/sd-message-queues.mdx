## What you'll learn

- Decouple a producer from a consumer, and name the three things the decoupling costs.
- Choose delivery semantics knowing that "exactly once" is not one of the options.
- Size a consumer pool, and read queue depth as a backlog with a drain time.
- Treat a dead-letter queue as work somebody has to do.

## Intuition

**A queue is a shared to-do list that deletes an item when somebody says they
have done it.** A producer appends work; the broker holds it durably; one of
several competing consumers takes an item, does it, and acknowledges. On that
acknowledgement the item is gone, and no other consumer ever sees it.

That one sentence contains most of the behaviour. Because a message is deleted
on acknowledgement, a queue fans out **work**, not information: ten consumers on
one queue do a tenth of the total each, and a second team that also wants those
events cannot have them. Because the broker holds the message durably, the
producer and the consumer no longer have to be healthy at the same moment.

What a queue solves is a step in a request path that the caller should not wait
for, or cannot: a video to transcode, an invoice to render, a welcome email to
send. It exists because the producer's deadline and the consumer's throughput
are two different numbers, and a buffer is the only honest way to hold them
apart. Decoupling happens in three dimensions at once — **time** (the consumer
may be restarting), **rate** (the producer may burst far above what the consumer
sustains) and **failure** (a consumer crash loses no work).

## How it works

The mechanism is a lease. A consumer does not remove a message when it receives
one; it takes it **invisible for a while** — the visibility timeout — and the
broker deletes it only on acknowledgement. If the acknowledgement does not
arrive before the lease expires, the message becomes visible again and somebody
else gets it.

```text
t=0    consumer A receives message M, lease 30 s
t=25   A is still working — a downstream call is slow
t=30   the lease expires; the broker makes M visible again
t=31   consumer B receives M and starts the same work
t=40   A finishes and acknowledges
       the work has now happened twice, and only the consumer's
       idempotency decides whether that mattered
```

Every delivery guarantee falls out of where the acknowledgement sits. Ack
**before** doing the work and you have at-most-once: a crash loses the message
silently. Ack **after** and you have at-least-once: a crash, a slow job or an
expired lease produces a duplicate. There is no third option end to end. What
gets sold as "exactly once" is at-least-once delivery plus a consumer whose
effect is idempotent, which
[Idempotency, Retries and Backpressure](/learn/sd-idempotency-and-backpressure/)
builds with a client-supplied key — so the message carries that key and the
consumer decides what a repeat means.

A message that fails every time needs somewhere to go. The broker counts
redeliveries, and after a threshold moves the message to a **dead-letter
queue**: a separate queue that nothing drains automatically. That is the point —
one poison message must not block the fleet, and it must not be discarded
either.

Keep the message small and let it name the work rather than carry it:

```json
{
  "message_id": "01J8Z3K5Q7V2",
  "type": "invoice.render",
  "order_id": "8812",
  "idempotency_key": "invoice-order-8812",
  "attempt": 3,
  "enqueued_at": "2026-08-26T09:14:02Z"
}
```

A message may be delivered minutes after it was written, so a copy of the order
inside it is a copy that can be stale. An identifier the consumer re-reads is
current by construction.

Now size the consumers, and take the backlog seriously.

```text
Assumptions
  peak arrivals        2,000 messages/s
  daily average          600 messages/s
  one message costs       50 ms of consumer time
  one consumer thread   1 ÷ 0.05  =  20 messages/s

Threads
  2,000 ÷ 20                    =  100 threads to exactly break even
  target ~70% utilisation       =  150 threads  (3,000 messages/s)
  running at 100% is where queueing time climbs steeply

A 10-minute downstream outage, consumers completing nothing
  2,000 × 600 s                 =  1,200,000 messages waiting

Draining, once arrivals fall back to the daily average
  spare capacity  3,000 − 600   =  2,400 messages/s
  1,200,000 ÷ 2,400             =  500 s, call it 8 minutes
```

The last two lines are the reason queue depth is a first-class metric: a
ten-minute incident produced eight further minutes of lateness after it ended.
Alarm on the **age of the oldest message** rather than the count — a hundred
thousand messages that are two seconds old is a healthy system, and a hundred
messages that are an hour old is a stuck one.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **A dead-letter queue is an operational commitment, not a setting.** Enabling
  it moves failures out of sight; nothing rescues those messages unless somebody
  looks. It needs a named owner, an alarm that fires when its depth leaves zero,
  and a replay path that is safe to run — which it is only if consumers are
  idempotent.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Use a queue when the caller does not need the result**, when a burst has to be
absorbed by something cheaper than more capacity, or when a step is allowed to
fail and be retried without the user noticing.

**Do not use one when the caller needs the answer now.** A queue in a
synchronous read path adds an enqueue, a poll and two failure modes to a call
that could have been a function. Do not use one when order across all items is
required: strict global ordering means one message in flight at a time, which
is the throughput of a single consumer. And do not use one when the work is
cheaper than the message — publishing, storing and redelivering costs more than
a millisecond of CPU.

The standing costs are three. You lose the synchronous error path: the producer
gets an acknowledgement from the broker, not from the work, so the user needs a
status they can poll and you need a way to answer "did it happen?". You lose the
stack trace, because the failure is now in a different process at a different
time. And at-least-once pushes idempotency into every consumer, permanently.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating the queue as unlimited.** It is a buffer, and a buffer with no
  bound converts overload into unbounded lateness. Tempting because a full queue
  looks like a failure and an infinite one looks like resilience.
- **A dead-letter queue nobody reads.** It is the tidiest way to lose data:
  every dashboard stays green while the messages accumulate.
- **Retrying a poison message forever.** A malformed payload will fail
  identically on the thousandth attempt, and meanwhile it is consuming the
  capacity that healthy messages need.
- **Putting the whole entity in the message.** It looks efficient — no extra
  read — and it means a late delivery applies yesterday's values.
- **Assuming order.** Competing consumers, redelivery and retries all reorder
  work; anything that depends on sequence needs a key that keeps it together, or
  a design that does not care.

## Interview notes

Whenever you draw a queue, say three things in the same breath: the delivery
semantics, how the consumer is made idempotent, and what the user sees while the
work is pending. "The upload enqueues a transcode job, the worker is idempotent
on the asset id so a redelivery is a no-op, and the client polls a status
endpoint" is a design. "We put it on a queue" is a box.

Then say what happens when the consumers cannot keep up, because that is the
question that separates people who have run one from people who have drawn one.
Queue depth, oldest-message age, a dead-letter queue with an owner, and the
drain arithmetic above are the whole answer.

## Key takeaways

- A queue **deletes a message on acknowledgement**, so it distributes work
  among competing consumers and cannot replay it to anybody else.
- Delivery is **at-most-once or at-least-once**; "exactly once" end to end is
  at-least-once plus an idempotent consumer.
- The visibility timeout is the whole mechanism: **a lease that expires
  mid-job produces a duplicate**, so leases must exceed the slowest realistic
  run.
- **Queue depth is a backlog with a drain time** — alarm on the age of the
  oldest message, and size consumers below full utilisation.
- A **dead-letter queue needs an owner, an alarm and a replay path**, or it is
  a place data goes to be forgotten.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A consumer's visibility timeout is 30 seconds, and about 1% of jobs legitimately take 90 seconds because of a slow third-party call. What happens to those jobs, and what are the fixes?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Each of those jobs is redelivered while it is still running: at 30 seconds the
  lease expires, a second consumer starts the same work, and at 60 seconds a
  third joins. The slowest 1% therefore run three times and the third-party call
  happens three times — with the first consumer's acknowledgement at 90 seconds
  deleting a message two others are still working on. There are three real
  fixes. Raise the timeout above the slowest realistic run, which delays
  legitimate redelivery after a crash by the same amount. Have the consumer
  extend its lease with a heartbeat while it works, which keeps fast redelivery
  for genuine failures. Or split the job so no single unit takes 90 seconds.
  Whichever you choose, the consumer still has to be idempotent on the
  third-party call, because a redelivery is always possible.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A colleague proposes: "we get exactly-once processing by acknowledging only after the work has committed." What does that actually give you?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  It gives you at-least-once, which is the right choice — but not exactly-once.
  Acknowledging after the commit removes one failure mode: work can no longer be
  lost by a crash between receiving and doing. It cannot remove the other one,
  because the commit and the acknowledgement are two separate steps in two
  separate systems. A crash after the commit and before the acknowledgement
  leaves work that is done and a message that will be redelivered, and no
  reordering of those two steps helps: put the acknowledgement first and you
  lose work instead. The property that makes the duplicate harmless is on the
  consumer's side — an idempotency key stored in the same transaction as the
  effect, so the second delivery finds the work already recorded and returns
  without repeating it.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. After a bad deploy, a dead-letter queue holds 40,000 messages. What has to happen before any of them is replayed?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  First, the bug has to be fixed and deployed, or a replay only refills the
  dead-letter queue. Second, somebody has to look at a sample and confirm the
  40,000 have one cause: a dead-letter queue collects everything that exceeded
  its retry limit, so a bad deploy's messages may be sitting alongside genuinely
  malformed payloads that will never succeed, and those need discarding rather
  than replaying. Third, check that the messages are still meaningful — if they
  carry entity identifiers the consumer re-reads, they are current; if they
  carry copies of state, some of that state has since changed and replaying
  applies stale values. Finally, replay at a controlled rate into the live
  queue. Forty thousand messages injected at once is a self-inflicted burst on
  top of normal traffic, and it is the second outage that usually gets
  remembered.
</PracticeCheck>
