## What you'll learn

- Name the four workload dials that account for most of the differences between the three platforms.
- Trace each dial to the mechanism it forces, and say when that mechanism is wrong.
- Tell a difference of size from a difference of shape.

## Intuition

The three columns of the comparison table are not three schools of thought. No
team chose prepositioning because it preferred it, or added a
candidate-generation stage because it was fashionable.

**An architecture is a function, and the workload is its argument.** Feed the
same requirement — "a user presses play and media arrives" — three different
workloads, and three different architectures come out. Taste has room only where
the function is flat.

Four dials account for most of it, all measurable before anything is built:

- **Item shape** — bytes per item, and items per session.
- **Catalogue shape** — how many items exist, and how fast that grows.
- **Popularity shape** — a concentrated head, or a long tail nobody can predict.
- **Write shape** — what generates the writes, and whether two of them collide.

Two modifiers sit behind them: **trust**, a property of who produces the
content, and the **latency floor** the product promises. Every derived row in the
table is downstream of these six.

## How it works

Take the dials one at a time, each to the mechanism it forces. Except where a
source is named, the mechanisms below are _generally accepted industry
architecture_, not claims about any company's internals.

**Item shape decides which resource binds.** Two quantities come out of a day of
use, and they move in opposite directions.

```text
bytes per daily user   = bytes per item  x  items per day
starts per daily user  = items per day  (each one is authorise, look up, sign)

Streaming      2 h in 4 items      0.3 Mbps per daily user,   4 starts
Music          1 h in 20 items     0.005 Mbps per daily user, 20 starts
Video sharing  40 min in 8 items   0.07 Mbps per daily user,   8 starts
```

A long item amortises its start cost over hours of bytes, so the delivery network
is the whole design and the application tier is unremarkable — a few thousand
queries per second (QPS). A short item repeats the start work every three
minutes, which is how a modest 230 Gbps platform still needs 10,000 plays per
second at average and 30,000 at peak. **Use adaptive bitrate** when items are
long enough that the network changes mid-item; **do not** pay for it on a 3 MB
audio file, where picking one of three renditions at start is enough and every
extra rendition costs catalogue-wide storage.

**Catalogue shape decides where metadata lives and whether ranking needs a
funnel.** Tens of thousands of items are a few hundred megabytes of metadata: hold
it in memory, and score the whole catalogue per user. A hundred million or a
billion items cannot be scored per request inside a latency budget, so
recommendation splits into cheap candidate generation over everything and
expensive ranking over a few hundred survivors — the structure the 2016 RecSys
paper _Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations_ (Covington, Adams,
Sargin) describes publicly, and the one the
[video-sharing recommender](/learn/sd-youtube-recommendations/) is built on. **Use a candidate stage** when scoring the corpus
cannot fit the budget; **do not** add one below roughly a hundred thousand items,
where it buys nothing and imposes a recall ceiling — anything it misses is
unreachable however good the ranker.

**Popularity shape decides the caching strategy, and with it the origin fleet.**
Origin capacity is not a function of traffic; it is a function of the traffic that
misses.

```text
origin fill = peak egress  x  miss ratio

  90 Tbps peak, 99% hit ratio   =  0.9 Tbps of origin fill
  90 Tbps peak, 95% hit ratio   =  4.5 Tbps of origin fill
```

Four percentage points of hit ratio are a fivefold difference in the fleet behind
the caches — the arithmetic
[Scale, Reliability and Degradation](/learn/sd-netflix-reliability/) opens with.
A curated catalogue with a schedule someone controls can push copies out before
demand arrives, keeping that ratio high. A long tail cannot:
most items are watched almost never, no cache can hold them, and a video can go
from cold to hot without warning. **Preposition** when the catalogue fits near
viewers and the schedule is known; **do not** when the tail is most of the
catalogue, because filling caches with items nobody requests evicts the head that
pays for them. There, delivery is pull-through caching over tiered storage, and
the design question becomes how fast a suddenly hot item fills.

**Write shape decides which consistency argument you are obliged to have.** Three
generators produce three different problems.

| What generates the writes | Rate scales with | The problem it creates                         |
| ------------------------- | ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| A person acting           | daily users      | Volume, usually modest; partition by owner     |
| A stream already running  | concurrency      | Sustained volume with nobody touching a device |
| A crowd on one object     | popularity       | Contention on a few hot keys                   |

Playlist edits are the first: about 1,000 writes per second, and the hard part is
that two devices and a collaborator may edit the same list, so operations are
synchronised rather than documents. Playback positions are the second: roughly
240,000 writes per second, generated by streams rather than by taps, which rules
out a single relational primary and makes the heartbeat interval the main knob.
View and like counters are the third: the difficulty is not the total but
thousands of increments landing on one row. **Use an append-only event stream,
aggregated in windows**, for anything displayed: an event id makes a redelivery
harmless, and a replay restates history when the counting rule
changes. **Sharded counters** summed on read are the simpler alternative above a
few hundred writes a second on one key, but they can neither deduplicate a
redelivery nor restate history — so **do not** count a number that pays somebody
on one. There the answer is that same log, deduplicated on a client-generated
event id.

<Callout variant="note">
  **Trust is a workload input, not a policy layer.** Who produces the content
  decides whether moderation sits *in* the publish path. With curated ingest the
  review happened once, at licensing time, and nothing blocks publication. With
  continuous user upload, abuse, spam and copyright matching are steps between
  upload and playable — which is why publish latency competes with encode
  quality on that column and on no other.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Designing from the workload over-fits to today's workload.** Every dial moves:
a music platform adding video podcasts has changed its item shape, a streaming
platform adding live events has changed its latency floor. Write the dial
position into the decision, so a review knows what would invalidate it.

**Shape tells you which mechanisms belong, not how many machines.** The chains
above are qualitative: only the estimate says whether the answer is one node, one
tier or a fleet, which is why this reasoning sits after
[Back-of-Envelope Estimation](/learn/sd-capacity-estimation/) rather than instead
of it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Copying a mechanism across a dial change.** Prepositioning, a candidate-free
  ranker and an in-memory catalogue are all correct on a small curated catalogue
  and all wrong on an unbounded one.
- **Confusing size with shape.** A smaller platform of the same shape needs the
  same mechanisms and fewer machines; a different shape needs different
  mechanisms at any size.
- **Answering a contention problem with capacity.** Adding write throughput does
  nothing for thousands of increments queued on one row.
- **Treating trust as something to add later.** Moderation in the publish path
  changes the pipeline, the storage lifecycle and the latency promise; it is not
  a service you attach afterwards.
- **Turning a dial you do not have.** Building for a billion-item catalogue while
  holding fifty thousand items buys a recall ceiling and a partitioning project
  for nothing.

## Interview notes

When asked why one platform does something another does not, answer with the
dial rather than the company: "their catalogue is four orders of magnitude
larger, so scoring everything per request does not fit the latency budget, so
ranking needs a candidate stage." That sentence is transferable; "because
YouTube-like platforms do it that way" is not.

The same move handles a change of requirements mid-interview: say which dial
moved, then walk the chain: "if users can upload, popularity stops being
predictable, so prepositioning stops paying, so the origin has to absorb the
misses, so storage tiers and moderation enters the publish path."

## Key takeaways

- **Item shape decides the binding resource**: long items make bytes bind, short
  items make request rate bind, at the same hours of media per user.
- **Catalogue shape decides the recommender**: a candidate stage is mandatory over
  a billion items and a recall ceiling for nothing over fifty thousand.
- **Origin capacity is peak egress times miss ratio** — so popularity
  predictability, not traffic, sizes the fleet.
- **Write shape names the consistency argument**: people, streams and crowds
  create volume, sustained volume and contention respectively.
- **Trust and the latency floor are workload inputs**, and they move whole
  pipelines rather than single components.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. Two products deliver about the same hour of media per user per day, yet one design is dominated by bandwidth and the other by request rate. Which dial explains it, and what does each design spend its effort on?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Item shape — the same hour split into different numbers of items, at different
  bitrates. An hour of long-form video is about two starts and 0.14 Mbps per
  daily user; an hour of music is twenty starts and 0.005 Mbps — a thirtyfold
  gap that is the bitrate ratio, not the hours. The bandwidth-bound design
  spends its effort below the application tier: segment sizes, adaptive bitrate,
  cache residency, origin fill, and keeping every application server off the
  byte path. The request-bound design spends it on the per-start path: an
  entirely cached catalogue so metadata never reaches a database, an
  authorisation and rights check cheap enough to run 30,000 times a second at
  peak, and a signed URL that lets delivery proceed without a further round
  trip.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A product's catalogue grows from 50,000 items to 500 million, and no other dial moves. Name three things in the architecture that must change, and one that need not.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Metadata storage changes first: a few hundred megabytes held in memory by
  every service becomes a terabyte or more, which needs a partitioned store with
  a cache in front rather than a copy per process. Recommendation changes
  second: scoring the whole catalogue per request no longer fits a latency
  budget, so ranking splits into candidate generation over everything and
  ranking over a few hundred survivors. Search changes third — a linear scan or
  a small index becomes a distributed inverted index with its own freshness lag.
  What need not change is delivery: bytes per item and items per session are
  untouched by the row count, so segment sizes, the rendition ladder and the
  byte path stay as they were. Storage volume grows, but that is a purchase, not
  a redesign.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. An engineer says view counters are hard because there are so many of them, and points out that the platform's aggregate counter rate is lower than its listening-event rate. Both statements are true. What is actually hard here?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  The distribution, not the total. A listening-event stream spreads across
  hundreds of millions of independent keys, so an append-only log partitions it
  cleanly and every partition sees a fraction of the load. Counter increments on
  a trending item all land on one key, so no partitioning scheme spreads them:
  the row becomes a serialisation point, and lock contention shows up at a rate
  far below the aggregate the platform handles elsewhere. That is why the answer
  is structural rather than capacity — shard the counter into many sub-counters
  and sum them on read, or aggregate increments in a window and apply them in
  batches, accepting a count that is seconds stale. Both trade exact,
  read-your-writes counting for the ability to absorb a hot key, which is the
  right trade for a displayed number and the wrong one for a number that pays
  somebody.
</PracticeCheck>
