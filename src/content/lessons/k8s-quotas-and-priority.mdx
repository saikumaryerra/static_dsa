## What you'll learn

- Cap a namespace with a `ResourceQuota` and supply defaults with a
  `LimitRange`.
- Explain why a quota rejection never produces a `Pending` Pod.
- Tell preemption, node-pressure eviction and API-initiated eviction apart.

## Intuition

A cluster is a shared pool, and sharing needs two different kinds of rule.

**A quota is a budget; a priority is a queue position.** The budget is checked
before anything is created: a namespace has been promised so much and may not
ask for more, whoever is asking and however urgent it is. The queue position
applies once everyone is inside the budget and the cluster still cannot hold
them all — someone has to go first, and someone has to wait.

The two act at different moments, which is the source of most confusion here. A
quota is enforced by **admission**, when the object is written. A Pod that
breaks a quota is never created at all, so nothing is ever `Pending` and nothing
appears in `kubectl get pods`. Priority is enforced by **the scheduler**, long
after the Pod exists.

And priority has a second, sharper power. When a high-priority Pod cannot be
placed anywhere, the scheduler may remove lower-priority Pods to make room. That
is preemption, and it is worth being precise about, because it is one of three
separate ways a Pod can be deleted by the system rather than by you.

## How it works

A `ResourceQuota` caps totals inside one namespace. It counts compute, storage
and object counts:

```yaml
apiVersion: v1
kind: ResourceQuota
metadata:
  name: team-budget
  namespace: payments
spec:
  hard:
    requests.cpu: '8'
    requests.memory: 16Gi
    limits.cpu: '16'
    limits.memory: 32Gi
    pods: '40'
    count/deployments.apps: '15'
    persistentvolumeclaims: '10'
```

Exceed one and the create request fails with a `403` naming the quota and the
resource. The Pod does not exist afterwards, which is why a Deployment that
cannot fit its replicas shows the failure on the ReplicaSet rather than on any
Pod:

```bash
kubectl describe quota team-budget -n payments
kubectl describe rs web-7d9f -n payments
```

<Callout variant="warning">
  A quota on `requests.cpu` or `requests.memory` makes those fields
  **mandatory** for every Pod in the namespace: with no request there is nothing
  to charge the budget, so the API rejects the Pod. Adding a quota to a busy
  namespace is therefore a change to every manifest in it — unless a
  `LimitRange` supplies the missing numbers first, as [the namespaces
  lesson](/learn/k8s-namespaces-and-kubectl/) warned.
</Callout>

A `LimitRange` is the other half. It fills in defaults and bounds each container:

```yaml
apiVersion: v1
kind: LimitRange
metadata:
  name: container-defaults
  namespace: payments
spec:
  limits:
    - type: Container
      default:
        cpu: 500m
        memory: 512Mi
      defaultRequest:
        cpu: 100m
        memory: 128Mi
      max:
        cpu: '4'
        memory: 8Gi
      min:
        cpu: 10m
        memory: 16Mi
```

`defaultRequest` and `default` are applied at admission to any container that
did not set them, and `min`/`max` reject the ones that set something outside the
band. The values are stamped into the Pod as it is created, so editing a
`LimitRange` changes nothing about the Pods already running — the next rollout
picks up the new numbers.

**Priority is a number on a cluster-scoped object**, referenced by name:

```yaml
apiVersion: scheduling.k8s.io/v1
kind: PriorityClass
metadata:
  name: payments-critical
value: 100000
globalDefault: false
preemptionPolicy: PreemptLowerPriority
description: 'Checkout path. May displace batch work.'
```

A Pod sets `spec.priorityClassName: payments-critical`, and admission copies the
value into `spec.priority`. That number does two things. It orders the
scheduler's queue, so a high-priority Pod is considered before a low-priority
one that has been waiting longer. And it enables preemption, unless the class
sets `preemptionPolicy: Never` — a useful combination for a Pod that deserves to
be considered early but should never displace anybody.

**Preemption, precisely.** When a Pod cannot be scheduled, the scheduler looks
for a node where deleting one or more lower-priority Pods would make it fit. It
picks the node that causes the least damage, deletes those victims through a
normal graceful termination, and records `status.nominatedNodeName` on the
pending Pod. The nomination is a hint, not a reservation: by the time the space
exists another Pod may have taken it, and the pending Pod is scheduled again
from scratch.

<Callout variant="note">
  Three different mechanisms delete a running Pod, and calling them all
  "eviction" is how teams end up debugging the wrong one. **Node-pressure
  eviction** is the kubelet reclaiming memory or disk on its own node, ranking
  its victims by whether a Pod is over its requests, then by Pod priority, then
  by how far over it is — so a PriorityClass decides who the kubelet kills as
  well as who the scheduler displaces, and QoS class is the shape that ranking
  produces rather than the key it sorts on. **Preemption** is the scheduler
  making room for a specific pending Pod, ordered by priority. **API-initiated
  eviction** is a client — a drain, an autoscaler — asking politely, and it is
  the only one a [PodDisruptionBudget](/learn/k8s-disruption-budgets/) can
  refuse.
</Callout>

Priority also reaches back into quota. A quota's `scopeSelector` can restrict it
to Pods of particular priority classes, which is how a cluster stops every team
from labelling its own work critical: the high-priority classes get a small
budget of their own.

## Trade-offs

**A quota moves the failure earlier and makes it louder.** Instead of a cluster
that gradually fills up, you get a clear rejection at the moment someone asks
for more than their share, addressed to the person who can act. The cost is real
friction — a legitimate scale-up fails at three in the morning because a budget
set six months ago was never revisited, and quota is the one control where the
error message is in a place people do not look.

**Priority is a scarce currency and it inflates.** It works when a small number
of workloads are genuinely more important than the rest. As soon as every team
may pick their own number, everything is critical, the ordering means nothing,
and the only lasting effect is that batch jobs are killed unpredictably. Whoever
runs the cluster should own the set of classes.

**Preemption buys latency for the important thing at the cost of predictability
for everything else.** A batch job that can be restarted is a good victim. A job
holding an hour of uncheckpointed work is not, and `preemptionPolicy: Never` on
the high-priority class is the honest choice when you cannot tell.

## Common pitfalls

- **Looking for a `Pending` Pod after a quota rejection.** There is no Pod. Read
  the ReplicaSet, the Job or the events in the namespace instead.
- **Adding a compute quota without a `LimitRange`.** Every Pod without explicit
  requests stops being admitted, including the ones that were running fine
  yesterday, the next time anything recreates them.
- **Editing a `LimitRange` and expecting running Pods to change.** The defaults
  were written into each Pod at creation. Nothing rewrites them.
- **`globalDefault: true` on a class that preempts.** It is tempting as a
  convenience and it makes every Pod in the cluster, including throwaway
  debugging Pods, a potential preemptor.
- **Assuming a PodDisruptionBudget stops preemption.** The scheduler tries to
  respect one, and will preempt anyway if that is the only way to place the Pod.

## Key takeaways

- **A quota is enforced at admission** — an over-budget Pod is rejected, never
  created, and never `Pending`.
- A compute quota **makes requests mandatory** in that namespace; a
  `LimitRange` supplies defaults and bounds, stamped in at creation.
- `PriorityClass` sets a number that both **orders the scheduling queue** and
  **enables preemption**, which `preemptionPolicy: Never` turns off.
- **Preemption is not eviction.** The scheduler removes lower-priority Pods to
  place one pending Pod; the kubelet evicts under node pressure by over-request
  and then priority; a drain evicts through the API.
- A preempted Pod gets its **normal graceful termination**, and the preemptor's
  `nominatedNodeName` is a hint rather than a reservation.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A team's Deployment is set to `replicas: 12`. Only 7 Pods exist, `kubectl get pods` shows nothing pending, and there are no `FailedScheduling` events. Where do you look?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  At the ReplicaSet and at the namespace's quota — `kubectl describe rs` and
  `kubectl describe quota`. A quota is enforced by admission, so the five
  missing Pods were rejected when the ReplicaSet controller tried to create them
  and no Pod object was ever written. That is precisely why there is nothing
  pending and no scheduling event: the scheduler never saw them, because they
  did not exist. The ReplicaSet records the failure as a `FailedCreate` event
  with the quota's message, and the Deployment carries a `ReplicaFailure`
  condition. The fix is either a smaller replica count, smaller requests, or a
  larger budget — whichever the team can defend.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A batch Pod running a two-hour job disappears with no node problem and no OOM kill. A high-priority Pod appeared on the same node a moment earlier. What happened, and what would have prevented it?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  It was preempted. The high-priority Pod could not be placed anywhere, the
  scheduler found a node where removing this lower-priority Pod would make room,
  and it deleted it — with a normal grace period, which is why it looks like an
  ordinary termination rather than a kill. Note what this is not: the node was
  healthy, so it was not node-pressure eviction, and nothing asked through the
  eviction API, so no PodDisruptionBudget was ever consulted. The preventions
  are on the other Pod's side: `preemptionPolicy: Never` on the high-priority
  class lets it queue first without displacing anyone, or the batch work gets a
  priority of its own rather than the default of zero. Checkpointing the job is
  the answer that survives whatever the cluster decides.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. Your namespace has a quota on `requests.cpu`. A colleague adds a container with no `resources` block at all and it is rejected. They point out that the same container has run for a year in another namespace. Reconcile the two facts.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Both are true, and the difference is the quota. A CPU quota has to charge
  every Pod against the budget, and a container with no CPU request offers
  nothing to charge — so rather than admitting an unbounded Pod into a bounded
  namespace, admission rejects it. The other namespace has no quota, so an
  unspecified container is admitted and lands in the Burstable or BestEffort
  class instead. The repair is either explicit requests on the container, which
  is the better habit anyway, or a `LimitRange` in this namespace with a
  `defaultRequest` so unspecified containers are given a number at creation.
  Note the second one changes nothing for Pods already running: the default is
  applied as the Pod is created.
</PracticeCheck>
