## What you'll learn

- Separate the three cost centres by their shape, and name the lever that moves each.
- Apply erasure coding and tiering to an exabyte without breaking playback latency.
- Say what a lost region does to each subsystem, in order.
- Build a degradation ladder that decides what is switched off first.

## Intuition

**Three costs, three shapes.** Bandwidth is a rate set by how much people watch,
so it plateaus when the audience does. Transcoding is a rate set by how much is
uploaded. Storage is neither: it is the **integral** of every upload ever
accepted, so it grows on flat traffic, on falling traffic, and on no traffic at
all. A capacity plan that treats all three as "big numbers that go up" will size
the first two correctly and be wrong about the third every year.

The second model splits the catalogue by which problem it is. **The head is where
the risk lives; the tail is where the money lives.** A million hot videos are
around a thousandth of the bytes and about 90% of the viewing, so they drive
delivery capacity, spikes and blast radius. The other billion are almost all of
the bytes and almost none of the viewing, so they drive the storage bill and
nothing else. Mixing the two up produces expensive reliability for content nobody
watches.

The third, for the second half of the lesson: **a region is a unit of correlated
failure**, and "what happens when we lose one" has a different answer per
subsystem. Answering it in general produces a slogan; answering it per subsystem
produces a plan.

## How it works

```text
Cost shapes (from the module estimate)
  Bandwidth    36 Tbps average, 100 Tbps peak   a rate, set by viewing
  Transcoding  180,000 cores, continuous        a rate, set by uploads
  Storage      ~1 EB now, +3 PB/day             an integral, never decreases
```

**1. Bandwidth.** Three levers, in order of size. A newer codec carries the same
picture in fewer bits and is paid for in encode compute — the only lever that
reduces delivered bytes without the viewer seeing a difference. Serving the right
rung comes next: a phone handed a rendition its screen cannot show is waste.
Third, caches change **where** the bytes come from rather than how many there
are — a better hit ratio removes origin reads and transit, not edge egress.
Confusing those two is the most common error in this arithmetic.

**2. Transcoding.** The ladder is the cost, and most of it is spent on videos
nobody watches. Encoding one modest rendition on publish and generating the
higher rungs only once a video crosses a view threshold moves the great majority
of that compute onto the small fraction that earns it. The remaining tail work is
interruptible, so it can run on preemptible capacity, which the idempotent worker
design already permits.

**3. Storage, where the biggest lever is.** Start with the multiplier that hides
at the end of every estimate:

```text
Physical storage (teaching assumptions)
  logical catalogue                          =  1 EB
  three full replicas                        =  3 EB
  erasure coding at ~1.4x overhead           =  1.4 EB
  saved by the coding scheme alone           ≈  1.6 EB
```

Erasure coding splits an object into data and parity fragments spread across
failure domains, surviving the loss of several at about 1.4 times the raw size
instead of three times. It costs more CPU and a reconstruction step when a
fragment is missing, which is why it suits large, immutable, read-mostly objects
— an exact description of a video segment.

Then tier by access. Hot content sits on fast storage, warm on cheaper media,
cold on the slowest. **The constraint that shapes the whole scheme:** any video
may be clicked at any moment, so nothing can sit on a tier that takes minutes to
respond. The resolution, _generally accepted_ across the industry, is to split by
rendition rather than by video — keep one low rung of everything warm enough to
start playback immediately, and push the high rungs and the source master to the
cold tier.

**4. Hot videos and headroom.** The global peak is about three times average, but
a single video's peak can be a thousand times its average, and cache tiers cannot
be provisioned in the ten minutes that takes. Spikes are absorbed by headroom
that is already deployed and by the request coalescing from the
[playback lesson](/learn/sd-youtube-playback/) — not by autoscaling, which is too
slow for the only case that matters.

**5. Losing a region.** Take a region as the correlated failure domain and answer
per subsystem:

| Subsystem      | What a lost region does                                                       |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Playback edges | Traffic steers to surviving regions; survivable only if headroom was reserved |
| Metadata       | Reads served by replicas elsewhere; writes wait for a promoted primary        |
| Upload         | Sessions in flight break; clients resume against another region               |
| Transcoding    | The queue is durable, so nothing is lost and publish latency rises            |
| Counters       | Events buffer in the stream; totals go stale, then catch up on replay         |
| Comments       | Writes fail over; thread ordering must not reissue a sequence value           |
| Search         | The index is a replicated read-only artifact, so other regions serve it       |
| Notifications  | Lowest priority: shed, retry, and accept lateness                             |

Two rows are worth pausing on. **Resumable upload is a disaster-recovery
mechanism**, not only a mobile-network one: a client that can ask "how many bytes
do you have?" can ask a different region. And the recovery point objective for an
accepted upload has to be zero, which holds only if the bytes were in more than
one failure domain before the acknowledgement was sent — the durability promise
from the requirements lesson, cashed.

<Callout variant="note">
  **A degradation ladder decides in advance what goes first.** Recommendations
  fall back to popular-in-locale rows, comments go read-only, counters freeze at
  their last value, uploads accept but do not publish, and playback is the last
  thing standing. Writing that order down before an incident is what stops it
  being improvised at three in the morning, and it is what makes partial failure
  a product decision rather than an accident. Netflix's public writing on chaos
  engineering is the _publicly documented_ example of testing these paths by
  injecting failure deliberately, and is what named the practice; it is now
  _generally accepted_. **When not to run one:** before the degradation paths
  exist and can be halted quickly, injecting failure is not an experiment, it is
  an outage.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Erasure coding against replication.** Coding roughly halves physical storage
and adds CPU and a reconstruction path on every degraded read. Replication is
simpler, faster to read from and more than twice the cost, which is why cold
objects are coded and hot metadata is replicated.

**Headroom against utilisation.** Running regions at high utilisation is
efficient until one is gone and the others cannot take the load. Sizing so that
the survivors absorb a full region means paying for capacity that is idle by
design.

**Cold tiering against retrieval latency.** Deeper tiers are dramatically cheaper
and answer in seconds or minutes, which no viewer will accept. Splitting by
rendition keeps the cheap tier available for the bytes nobody is waiting on.

## Common pitfalls

- **Quoting the daily growth as the storage figure.** Three petabytes a day is a
  rate; the bill is its integral, and it is already an exabyte.
- **Forgetting the replication multiplier.** Every logical estimate is short by
  the factor the storage layer applies, and that factor is a design choice worth
  making explicitly.
- **Counting a cache hit ratio as a bandwidth saving.** It saves origin reads and
  transit, and the bytes still leave an edge.
- **Sizing for all regions healthy.** Everything is comfortable until one is
  gone, and then the survivors fail in the same hour.
- **Failover that has never been exercised.** A promotion path nobody has run is
  a plan, not a capability, and incidents are a poor first rehearsal.

## Interview notes

Lead with the three shapes. "Bandwidth and transcoding are rates; storage is an
integral" is one sentence that reframes a cost conversation, and it is the reason
tiering and lazy encoding are architecture rather than housekeeping.

On failure, refuse the general answer and go subsystem by subsystem. Saying "the
transcode queue is durable so we lose latency, not work; uploads in flight break
but resume against another region; counters buffer in the stream and catch up"
demonstrates that each component's failure behaviour was designed rather than
assumed.

## Key takeaways

- Bandwidth and transcoding are **rates**; storage is an **integral** that grows
  even when nothing else does.
- The head drives **risk and delivery capacity**; the tail drives **cost**.
  Optimise each for the right thing.
- **Erasure coding plus rendition-level tiering** is the large storage lever, and
  it works because one low rung of everything stays warm.
- **Resumable upload is a recovery mechanism**, and an accepted upload's recovery
  point objective is zero only if the acknowledgement waited for a second failure
  domain.
- A **written degradation ladder** turns partial failure into a product decision;
  chaos testing is how you find out whether it works.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. Of bandwidth, transcoding and storage, which can be reduced substantially without any viewer noticing, and by what?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Storage, by a large factor, and it is the one nobody looks at. Two levers
  compound. Erasure coding instead of three full replicas takes the physical
  footprint from roughly three times the logical size to about 1.4 times, which
  on an exabyte of catalogue is over an exabyte and a half saved, at the cost of
  CPU and a reconstruction step on degraded reads. Tiering by rendition then
  attacks what is stored rather than how: keep one low rung of every video warm
  so any click starts immediately, and push the high rungs and the source master
  to cold storage, retrieving them only for videos that attract an audience.
  Since the tail is almost all of the bytes and almost none of the viewing, the
  viewers affected are the handful who watch a cold video in high resolution,
  and they wait once. Bandwidth can only be cut by delivering fewer or smaller
  bytes, which a viewer sees unless a better codec pays for it in encode compute
  — and transcoding is already bounded by what creators upload.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A region is lost at peak. Say what happens to an upload that is halfway through, to the transcoding backlog, and to view counts.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  The upload's session breaks, because the session URI was served by that region
  — but the protocol is what makes this recoverable. The client re-queries with
  `Content-Range: bytes */TOTAL` against a surviving region, is told how many
  bytes are already committed, and continues from there; the bytes already
  acknowledged are safe because they were committed to more than one failure
  domain before the acknowledgement was sent. The transcoding backlog loses no
  work at all: the queue is durable and the jobs are idempotent, so anything
  half-encoded is redelivered and redone elsewhere. What is lost is time —
  publish latency rises until the surviving fleet drains the queue, and if that
  fleet was sized without headroom it may not drain during peak. View counts
  stop advancing rather than going wrong: events accumulate in the stream, the
  displayed totals go stale, and when aggregation resumes it replays from the
  last committed offset and catches up. That staleness is exactly the property
  the counter design bought.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A proposal: move every video not watched in the past year to an archival tier whose retrieval takes minutes. On the numbers here it would archive most of the catalogue.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  The premise is right about the size of the prize — most of the catalogue is
  cold, so most of the bytes would move to the cheapest tier, and that is where
  the storage bill lives. The problem is that the tier's retrieval time is on
  the viewer's path. Any archived video is one click away from someone, and a
  click that produces minutes of waiting is not a degraded experience, it is a
  broken one; worse, the videos affected are exactly the obscure ones a search
  or a recommendation has surfaced, so the failure lands on discovery. Keep the
  proposal and change its unit from the video to the rendition. One low rung of
  every video stays on a tier that answers in milliseconds, which is a small
  fraction of the ladder's bytes, and the high rungs plus the source master go
  to archive. A viewer who opens a forgotten video starts playing immediately at
  modest quality, a background job restores the higher rungs if the video starts
  attracting attention, and almost all of the saving is still collected.
</PracticeCheck>
