## What you'll learn

- Design the audio path from an encoded object to a listener's speaker.
- Choose a rendition ladder, a prefetch policy and a client cache, and say what each costs.
- Separate the bytes, which are cacheable forever, from the rights, which are not.

## Intuition

**Audio delivery is a caching problem wearing a streaming problem's clothes.** A
three-minute track at 128 kbps is roughly 3 MB, smaller than the images on many
web pages, and once encoded it never changes. Nothing about that needs adaptive
segmentation, a manifest or a rebuffering strategy of the kind video demands. It
needs an immutable object, a URL that can be cached, and a client that fetches
the next one before the current one ends.

The second idea is the one that makes the design non-obvious. **The bytes are
public-shaped and the permission is private-shaped.** The same encoded file is
identical for every listener on earth, so it should be cached as close to
listeners as possible and kept for as long as possible. Whether a particular
listener may play it depends on their subscription, their country and a licensing
window that a label can close tomorrow. Push those two facts into one mechanism
and you get either uncacheable audio or unenforceable rights. Keep them apart —
**cache the object forever, authorise the access every time** — and both work.
This split is generally accepted industry architecture for licensed media, and
the Spotify-like platform below applies it; none of it is a claim about any
particular company's implementation.

## How it works

**Encoding.** A track arrives as a high-quality master and is transcoded once
into a small ladder — around 96 kbps for metered mobile connections, 128 kbps as
the default, 256 kbps for subscribers who ask for higher quality. Each rendition
is a separate immutable object in
[object storage](/learn/sd-object-storage/), named by a content hash so the name
changes if the bytes ever do. That is what makes the URL safe to cache
indefinitely.

Transcoding here is cheap and batchable, the opposite of the video case: three
minutes of audio at three bitrates is seconds of work on one core, so a new
release is encoded, checked and published without anybody designing a scheduling
system for it.

**Delivery.** The client asks the playback service to authorise a play and
receives short-lived signed URLs, one per rendition. It then fetches bytes from a
[content delivery network](/learn/sd-cdn/) with HTTP range requests, taking the
first few seconds first so playback can start, then the rest. The origin is
object storage, and it is reached only on a cache miss.

```text
request
  GET /audio/9f2ca1b7e4/128.ogg
  Range: bytes=0-131071

response
  206 Partial Content
  Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable
```

Cache efficiency is unusually good here for two reasons that are properties of
music rather than clever engineering. **Assume listening is concentrated**, a
small fraction of tracks taking most plays. And the catalogue is a petabyte that
barely grows day to day, so the head an edge holds stays the head — where the
video platform in [Back-of-Envelope Estimation](/learn/sd-capacity-estimation/)
adds 36 PB a year for a cache to chase. Assume a few terabytes of the most-played
audio in a large market — a sliver of the catalogue by bytes — and it serves the
large majority of that market's requests.

**Regional distribution** follows from the estimate: 230 Gbps of average egress
is a delivery-network purchase, not a datacentre project. A commercial CDN with
points of presence in each large market is enough. The publicly documented
extreme is **Netflix Open Connect**, which places Netflix's own caching
appliances inside internet service providers' networks. Assume a video platform
in the tens of terabits per second against the hundreds of gigabits derived here,
and a music platform's byte volume rarely justifies building and shipping
hardware.

**Prefetch.** The client knows what plays next, because a queue or an album is
an ordered list. Fetching the next track while the current one plays removes the
gap at a track change and hides a slow network entirely. When not to prefetch:
on a metered or roaming connection, where a skipped prefetch is wasted data the
listener pays for; and when the next item is genuinely unknown, as in a shuffled
radio stream whose next pick depends on a skip that has not happened yet. Then
prefetch a candidate and accept an occasional miss, or shorten the ladder instead.

**The client cache** holds recently played tracks under a size cap with
least-recently-used eviction, which removes the network from a repeat listen
altogether. It is stored encrypted, because a cache of playable audio files on
disk is a distribution channel if the bytes are readable.

**Offline** is the same mechanism with the dial turned up. A download is the same
encrypted object stored without an eviction policy, plus a **licence**: a small
signed record saying which content this device may decrypt and until when. The
client revalidates that licence periodically — a window of a few weeks is a
common teaching figure — and when the subscription lapses the licence is not
renewed, so downloads stop playing without anybody deleting anything. This is the
concept-level shape of digital rights management; the cryptographic detail is a
specialist subject and none of it changes the architecture.

**Rights** run through the same seam. A track's availability is per territory and
per licensing window, and a label can pull a track with short notice. Because
audio URLs are cached forever and cannot be recalled from every edge in the
world, revocation cannot be a cache operation. It is an authorisation operation:
the signed URL expires in minutes, so a track pulled at noon stops being
authorised at noon and the last listeners drop off as their URLs expire. The
cached bytes are then garbage that no one can request a signature for.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **A signed URL is a bearer token.** Anyone holding it can fetch the object
  until it expires, so the expiry is the security control — minutes, not days —
  and it should be bound to the rendition and, where the client allows, the
  session. Long-lived signed URLs are how a licensed catalogue leaks.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Immutable URLs against revocation.** Caching forever is what makes the
economics work, and it means you can never take a byte back. The cost is short
authorisation lifetimes — 10,000 authorisations per second, which the previous
lesson already sized for.

**Every rendition multiplies two things.** A fourth bitrate adds storage across a
hundred million tracks and, worse, splits cache occupancy: the same track now
occupies four entries competing for the same edge memory. Three renditions is a
defensible ladder for audio; the video habit of eight or ten does not transfer.

**Prefetch against data cost.** Gapless playback, paid for in bytes on tracks the
listener may skip — so it is a policy that reads the connection type, not a
constant.

## Common pitfalls

- **Proxying audio through the application tier.** It is the shortest path to a
  working prototype and it converts a bandwidth bill into an application fleet.
- **Expecting a cache TTL to enforce a takedown.** Purging every edge is slow and
  unreliable, and the object may already be on ten million phones. Enforce in
  authorisation, where the answer is computed fresh.
- **Caching the authorisation response.** The bytes are shared; the decision is
  personal. Cache the decision and one listener's territory or subscription
  decides for another's.
- **One object per track with no range support.** Playback then waits for a whole
  file before starting, and a seek re-downloads from the beginning.
- **Ignoring the cold cache in a new market.** The first hours in a new region
  are a miss storm against the origin; pre-warm the most-played tracks or accept
  a slow start, but decide which.

## Interview notes

One sentence does most of the work: "the bytes are identical for every listener
and cacheable forever, and the right to play them is personal and expires in
minutes." Say it first and the takedown question answers itself.

Then say what revocation actually is. "We purge nothing, we stop signing URLs,
and the last listeners drop off as their signatures expire" is a mechanism;
"we'd invalidate the CDN" is a wish — the object is already on ten million
phones.

## Key takeaways

- Audio objects are **small, immutable and identical for everyone**, so they are
  cached indefinitely under a content-addressed URL.
- Rights are **evaluated per request** and expressed as a short-lived signed URL,
  which is what makes a takedown effective without purging any cache.
- A **three-rendition ladder** is enough; each extra rendition costs storage
  across the whole catalogue and fragments edge cache occupancy.
- **Prefetch the next item** when it is known and the connection is not metered;
  a client cache removes the network from repeat listens.
- **Offline is a download plus an expiring licence**, so access ends by expiry
  rather than by deletion.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A label pulls a track from one country at noon. Cached copies exist at hundreds of edge locations and on millions of devices. How does the platform stop serving it, and how quickly?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Not by purging caches — that is slow, incomplete, and useless against copies
  already on devices. The catalogue's rights record for that territory is
  updated, and from that moment the playback service refuses to sign a URL for
  listeners in that country. Playback stops within the lifetime of the
  signatures already issued, which is minutes because that is what the expiry
  was chosen for. Listeners with an offline download keep it until their licence
  next revalidates, which is why the licence has an expiry at all. The cached
  bytes stay at the edges and become unreachable: without a valid signature no
  one can request them, and they age out normally.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A proposal adds two more bitrates to the ladder so every connection gets a closer match. Name what it costs beyond storage.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Cache occupancy, which is the expensive one. Five renditions means one track
  occupies five entries at every edge, so the number of distinct tracks a given
  cache can hold falls by roughly two fifths against three renditions, and the
  hit ratio falls with it — pushing traffic back to the origin, which is the
  cost the CDN existed to avoid. Encoding cost rises across a hundred million
  tracks, and every client and test matrix gains two more cases. The gain is a
  slightly better bitrate match on a medium that is already small enough to
  prefetch, which is why audio ladders stay short where video ladders do not.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A client team suggests caching the playback service's authorisation response for ten minutes per track, since a listener often replays a track. What breaks?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  The response is a decision about one listener at one moment, not a fact about
  the track. Cached on the client for that listener alone it is defensible but
  shortens nothing — the signature already expires in minutes, so the cache
  would mostly serve expired URLs. Cached anywhere shared, it is a rights
  failure: the entry carries a signature minted for one listener's subscription
  and territory, and a second listener served from it is playing on someone
  else's permission. The rule from the lesson holds — the bytes are shared and
  cacheable, the decision is personal and is not.
</PracticeCheck>
