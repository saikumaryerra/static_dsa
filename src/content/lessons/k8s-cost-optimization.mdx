## What you'll learn

- Explain why requests, and not usage, decide what a cluster costs.
- Find the three places money hides: idle requests, node shape, and forgotten objects.
- Pick a lever and say what it costs you in reliability.

## Intuition

Your provider bills you for machines. The scheduler decides how many machines
you need, and it decides that from
[requests](/learn/k8s-requests-and-limits/) — the amount each Pod asks to have
set aside. Once a Pod is placed, its request is unavailable to anyone else
whether the container uses it or not.

**You pay for reservations, not for meals.** A restaurant that holds a table for
four all evening has the same room to sell whether one person or four turns up.
The gap between what your workloads reserved and what they actually ran is not
waste in a metaphorical sense — it is a specific number of machines, and you can
count them.

Two consequences fall straight out. Limits are not reservations, so changing
a limit cannot change the bill: it changes what happens to your own container
when it exceeds one. And every conversation about Kubernetes cost that is not
about requests, node shapes or things nobody deleted is a conversation about
something else.

## How it works

### Measure the gap first

```text
Cluster:           20 nodes, 8 vCPU / 32 GiB each
Allocatable:       20 x 7.4 vCPU = 148 vCPU   (capacity minus kubelet and
                   20 x 29 GiB   = 580 GiB     system reserves)

Sum of requests:   118 vCPU   = 80% of allocatable
Peak actual use:    34 vCPU   = 23% of allocatable

Reserved and unused: 118 - 34 = 84 vCPU, roughly 11 nodes of a 20-node bill
```

Note the first line of arithmetic people forget: **allocatable is smaller than
capacity**. The kubelet, the system reserves and the eviction threshold take a
slice of every node before any Pod is placed, so a fleet of small nodes pays
that overhead more times than a fleet of large ones.

The two commands that produce those numbers:

```bash
kubectl describe node node-7
kubectl top pods --all-namespaces --sort-by=cpu
```

`describe node` reports allocated requests as a percentage of allocatable —
that is the reservation side. `kubectl top` reports real usage and needs
metrics-server, an ecosystem addon rather than part of Kubernetes. The gap
between the two is the subject of this lesson.

### Where the money is

**Idle requests** are usually the largest share. Most requests were set once
from a guess and never revisited, and guesses are generous because nobody wants
to be paged. A VerticalPodAutoscaler in recommendation mode watches real usage
and reports a suggested request without changing anything, which makes it a
measuring tool before it is an automation one; VPA is an addon, not core
Kubernetes.

**Node shape** strands capacity that no workload can claim. If your Pods request
1 vCPU and 8 GiB, an 8 vCPU / 32 GiB node — 7.4 vCPU and 29 GiB of it
allocatable — holds three of them rather than four, and 4.4 vCPU sit unusable
because memory ran out first. The largest single Pod also sets the
smallest node you may buy. Matching the node's CPU-to-memory ratio to your
workloads' ratio is often worth more than any autoscaler.

**Forgotten objects** cost quietly and forever: PersistentVolumes left
`Released` by a `Retain` reclaim policy, PersistentVolumeClaims that outlived
the StatefulSet that made them, snapshots that accumulate on a schedule with no
expiry, LoadBalancer Services from a demo, node pools with a minimum size
somebody set during an incident, and non-production clusters running through
every night and weekend.

### The levers, and when not to pull them

| Lever                                    | What it recovers                                                                      | When not to                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Right-size requests toward observed peak | the idle-request gap, usually the biggest                                             | when the peak is periodic (a nightly batch) or memory is a cliff rather than a curve            |
| Lower the HPA floor off-peak             | replicas nobody was served by                                                         | when a cold start is slower than the traffic ramps                                              |
| Cluster autoscaler, scale-to-zero pools  | whole machines                                                                        | when scale-up latency sits on a user's request path                                             |
| Spot or preemptible nodes                | a large share of node price, varying by provider                                      | for stateful workloads, long jobs, or anything without a disruption budget and topology spread  |
| Change the node shape                    | stranded CPU or memory                                                                | when the largest Pod stops fitting, or one node's failure becomes a big fraction of the cluster |
| Switch non-production off out of hours   | roughly two-thirds of that bill — a working week is about a third of the week's hours | when colleagues in another time zone rely on it                                                 |

### Give the number an owner

Nobody optimises what nobody owns. Attribute node cost to namespaces by their
share of requests, and a ResourceQuota stops being an abstract cap and becomes a
budget with a currency figure attached. Ecosystem tools such as OpenCost do that
attribution; nothing in Kubernetes does it for you, and a single cluster-wide
figure in a finance report is a number no engineer can act on.

<Callout variant="note">
  Right-sizing changes a Pod's QoS class. QoS is a property of the Pod: it is
  Guaranteed only when every container in it sets CPU and memory requests equal
  to its limits, and dropping any one request below its limit makes the whole
  Pod Burstable — which puts it earlier in the eviction order when a node runs
  short of memory. That is a fair trade for a stateless service with several
  replicas and a poor one for the workload that pages you.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Packing against headroom.** A cluster with requests equal to allocatable has
nowhere to put the Pods from a node that fails, and no room for a rollout that
briefly runs both versions. Every percentage point of extra packing is a
percentage point of the buffer that absorbs failure —
[Bottlenecks and Capacity Planning](/learn/k8s-capacity-planning/) is the
lesson on choosing that buffer deliberately.

**Automation against predictability.** An autoscaler that removes nodes at
03:00 saves money and means the cluster you debug at 09:00 is not the cluster
you left. Scale-to-zero is the strongest version of this: the cheapest possible
environment, and a first request that waits for a machine to boot.

**Speed against price.** Spot capacity is the clearest example — a real discount
in exchange for a node that can be reclaimed at short notice. It is close to
free money for stateless replicas spread across zones with a disruption budget,
and it is an outage generator for a database.

## Common pitfalls

- **Cutting limits instead of requests.** It looks like restraint and saves
  nothing: limits are not reserved and not billed, so the only effect is
  throttling or an OOM kill in your own container.
- **Right-sizing from averages.** The mean is the one number the workload is
  never at during an incident. Size on a high percentile of peak, then subtract
  confidence rather than capacity.
- **Reading low usage as no usage.** A namespace at 2% CPU may hold the thing
  that runs once a quarter. Ask before deleting; the saving is small and the
  apology is not.
- **Optimising the cluster and forgetting the storage.** Compute is elastic and
  visible, and volumes and snapshots keep billing at the same rate whether or
  not anything mounts them.

## Key takeaways

- The bill follows **requests**, because requests are what the scheduler
  reserves and what decides the node count. Limits change behaviour, never cost.
- **Allocatable is less than capacity**, and stranded capacity from a mismatched
  node shape is invisible in every per-Pod view.
- The three sources are **idle requests, unpackable node shapes and objects
  nothing deletes** — in that order, most of the time.
- Every lever trades money for a reliability property: headroom, start-up
  latency, eviction order, or the right to be reclaimed at short notice.
- Cost is only acted on when it is **attributed to a namespace with an owner**,
  and Kubernetes does none of that attribution itself.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A colleague proposes halving every container's memory limit across the cluster to reduce the bill. What will that actually achieve?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  No saving at all, and a new class of incident. The scheduler places Pods using
  *requests*, and the number of nodes you rent follows from the sum of requests
  — limits are never reserved on a node, so halving them frees nothing for
  anyone and changes no node count. What does change is what happens when a
  container bursts: memory above the limit is an OOM kill, so every workload
  whose peak sat between the old and new limit starts dying under exactly the
  load it used to survive. The proposal is right about wanting a smaller number
  and wrong about which number: the same effort spent lowering over-generous
  requests would move the bill.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. Using the worked example above — 20 nodes, 118 vCPU requested, 34 vCPU used at peak — a colleague concludes the cluster could be five nodes. What is correct in that, and what is missing?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Correct: real peak usage is about 34 vCPU, which does fit inside five of these
  nodes, so the workloads themselves are nowhere near needing twenty machines.
  Missing, in order of importance: nothing shrinks until the *requests* shrink,
  because the scheduler still needs somewhere to place 118 vCPU of reservations;
  memory may bind before CPU, so the same arithmetic has to be repeated on the
  memory column; five nodes leaves no headroom, so one node failure has nowhere
  to put the evicted Pods; and the largest single Pod still sets the smallest
  node you can buy. The realistic move is to right-size requests toward observed
  peak, keep at least a node's worth of spare capacity, and let the cluster
  autoscaler settle the count.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. You delete a StatefulSet with three replicas to save money, and the bill barely moves. Why?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Deleting a StatefulSet deletes its Pods, so you stopped paying for compute —
  which on most bills is not where a database-shaped workload's cost was. Its
  PersistentVolumeClaims are deliberately **not** deleted, so that recreating
  the StatefulSet reattaches the same data, and the three volumes carry on
  existing and being charged for. If the StorageClass reclaim policy is
  `Retain`, even deleting those claims leaves the PersistentVolumes and their
  backing disks behind in a `Released` state. The fix is to decide about the
  data explicitly: delete the claims once you are sure, check for released
  volumes afterwards, and set the StatefulSet's
  `persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy` in advance when the data is genuinely
  disposable.
</PracticeCheck>
