## What you'll learn

- Turn a service name into a healthy address, and bound how stale that answer is.
- Say what a consensus group costs per decision, and how many failures it takes.
- Recognise split brain, and fence against it instead of trusting a lease.
- Keep coordination out of the request path and out of the data path.

## Intuition

**Discovery is a naming problem. Coordination is an agreement problem.** They
are taught together because the same cluster usually provides both, and they are
worth separating because their costs differ by orders of magnitude.

Discovery answers "where is the payments service?" when instances start, stop,
move and fail continuously. A registry holds the current set of addresses and a
health signal, and hands out an answer. Crucially, **that answer is always a
little out of date** — an instance can die a microsecond after the registry
served its address — so discovery is a best-effort optimisation over a client
that must retry anyway.

Coordination answers "which of you is the leader?", "who holds this lock?",
"what is the current configuration?" — questions where several machines must
reach the **same** answer, and where two different answers is a defect rather
than staleness. That is consensus, and consensus is expensive: every decision
costs a round trip to a majority, and the group stops deciding entirely when it
loses one.

The design rule follows from the gap. Discovery is cheap and can be wrong for a
few seconds. Coordination is costly and must not be wrong, so put as little
through it as you can.

## How it works

**Registration and health.** An instance registers on start with its address and
metadata, refreshes a heartbeat, and deregisters on a clean shutdown. Instances
that die badly are removed when their heartbeat lapses. The window in which a
name resolves to a dead instance is a sum, and it is longer than people expect:
detection is interval × threshold plus one timeout, as
[Load Balancers and Reverse Proxies](/learn/sd-load-balancers/) sets out, plus
propagation to the registry, plus whatever the client has cached.

```text
health check every 5 s, timeout 1 s, 3 consecutive failures to eject
  detection                       =  5 × 3 + 1  =  16 s
  registry propagation to clients                ≈   5 s
  client-side cache or DNS TTL of 30 s           ≈  30 s
  worst case a client dials a dead instance      ≈  51 s
```

Nearly a minute is why every caller retries against another address, with backoff
and a budget. No registry setting removes that window; it only makes it shorter
and the health checks more expensive.

**Two shapes of discovery.** In _client-side_ discovery the caller asks the
registry for the set and picks an instance itself: one fewer hop and per-caller
balancing, at the price of a client library in every language. In _server-side_
discovery the caller sends to a stable address and a proxy resolves: one place
to operate, at the price of a hop in the path. DNS is the common middle, and it
inherits DNS's caching behaviour, including clients that ignore TTLs. All three
are generally accepted industry architecture rather than anybody's proprietary
design.

**Consensus, and its price.** A consensus algorithm makes a group of nodes agree
on an ordered sequence of decisions despite crashes and lost messages. Raft
(Ongaro and Ousterhout, 2014) is what most current systems implement; Paxos is
the older family it was designed to be more teachable than. The mechanism is a
majority: a decision commits when more than half the group has durably recorded
it.

```text
group of 5 nodes, majority = 3
  survives 2 failures and still decides
  loses 3 → the group cannot commit anything, including electing a leader

one decision = one round trip from the leader to a majority
  inside one datacentre   0.5 ms round trip  →  order of a few thousand
                                                serialised decisions/s
  across continents       150 ms round trip  →  single figures per second
  batching raises the throughput; it cannot lower the latency floor
```

That table is the argument for keeping coordination small. A store whose write
path is a majority round trip suits membership, leadership, locks and a few
kilobytes of configuration, and is hopeless as a database — and a group
stretched across regions pays the cross-continent floor on every decision, as
[Consistency Models, CAP and PACELC](/learn/sd-consistency-models/) describes.
Google's Chubby paper (Burrows, 2006) is a publicly documented example of this
shape: a lock service that, in practice, was used mostly as a name service and a
small configuration store.

**Leader election is consensus wearing a hat.** The group agrees on one value —
"node 3 leads until time T" — and that value is a **lease**, not a fact. The
danger is a leader that has not noticed it lost the lease: partitioned from the
group, or paused by garbage collection or a hypervisor stall, it wakes up still
believing it leads while a new leader is already working. Two leaders, both
sincere: **split brain**.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **Fencing tokens are the honest answer to split brain.** Each leadership term
  carries a number that only increases. The leader sends its token with every
  write to the shared resource; the resource remembers the highest token it has
  seen and rejects anything lower. The deposed leader's writes are then refused
  by the storage rather than prevented by good timing. The term is Kleppmann's
  (2017); the underlying lease mechanism is Gray and Cheriton's (1989). The
  limit is honest too — a resource that cannot check a token cannot be fenced,
  and then the only protection left is making the writes idempotent.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Use a registry** as soon as instances outnumber what a static list can track,
or as soon as they move — autoscaling, rolling deploys and failover all
invalidate hardcoded addresses. **Do not** put a registry in front of a fixed,
small set of endpoints; a configuration file that changes twice a year is
cheaper to operate and cannot go down.

**Use consensus** for the small set of facts where disagreement is a bug: who
leads, who holds a correctness-protecting lock, what the configuration is, which
members exist. **Do not** put it in the request path for ordinary data; its
capacity is one serialised decision stream and its dataset is expected to fit in
memory on every node.

The costs are specific. **Losing a majority stops the group completely**, where
a single node with a manual failover would be degraded rather than dead — and
redundancy only helps to the degree failures are independent, so five nodes in
one rack are five correlated nodes
([Availability and Durability](/learn/sd-availability-and-durability/)).
Distributed locks that are only an optimisation are worth having cheaply; locks
that protect correctness need fencing, or an idempotent resource, or they are
decoration.

## Common pitfalls

- **Trusting a resolved address.** The registry holds a cached opinion about
  liveness, so a client without retries and backoff turns every eviction window
  into user errors — and a runtime that caches an address for the whole process
  lifetime keeps dialling a machine retired at breakfast.
- **A registry with no fail-static behaviour.** When the registry is unreachable
  the correct move is to keep using the last known set; the tempting one is to
  resolve to nothing, which converts a registry outage into a total outage.
- **A restart herd.** Every instance re-registering and re-resolving at once
  after a deploy is a load spike aimed at the one component everything needs.
- **Treating a lock as a guarantee.** A held lease and a running process are not
  the same thing once a pause is possible, and the gap is where split brain
  lives.
- **Coordinating too much.** Every decision that goes through consensus inherits
  its latency and its quorum requirement, so a chatty use of it makes the whole
  system as available as the smallest quorum.

## Interview notes

Say the staleness out loud — a registry is an optimisation over a caller that
retries, not a source of truth, and quoting the eviction window shows you know
which.

For anything with a leader, name the failure and the fence in one sentence:
"leadership is a lease from the consensus group, and because a paused leader can
wake up believing it still leads, every write to the shared store carries a
fencing token the store checks." Then say what you deliberately did **not**
coordinate — that is the sentence that shows you know what it costs.

## Key takeaways

- **Discovery is best-effort naming**: the answer is always slightly stale, so
  clients retry and the eviction window is interval × threshold, plus one
  timeout, plus caching.
- **Consensus costs a majority round trip per decision** — a few thousand a
  second inside a datacentre, single figures across continents.
- A group of 5 **tolerates 2 failures and stops entirely at 3**; that is a real
  availability cost, not only a durability gain.
- **A leader holds a lease, not a fact.** A paused or partitioned leader that
  wakes up is split brain.
- **Fencing tokens** — monotonic per term, checked by the resource — are what
  make leadership safe; a resource that cannot check one cannot be fenced.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A service is behind a name with a 30-second DNS TTL, and instances are health-checked every 5 seconds with a 3-failure threshold. An instance dies. For how long can callers still be sent to it, and what should they do about it?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Add the windows rather than taking the largest. Detection is 5 × 3 = 15
  seconds plus the failing probe's own timeout before the instance is declared
  unhealthy, then the change has to propagate to whatever answers queries, and
  then a client that resolved the name a moment before the change keeps its
  cached answer for up to another 30 seconds — so roughly 45 to 50 seconds in
  the worst case, and longer for a client that caches beyond the TTL, which many
  runtimes do. Shortening the interval and the TTL narrows the window and costs
  more checks and more lookups; it never closes it, because a healthy instance
  can die immediately after passing a check. The behaviour that actually fixes
  it belongs to the caller: connect with a short timeout, retry the next address
  on a connection failure, and use a retry budget so a widespread failure does
  not become a retry storm.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A team runs a 5-node consensus cluster for leader election and configuration. They propose storing per-user session data in it too, "since it is already replicated and consistent". What do you tell them?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  That the properties they like are exactly what makes it the wrong store for
  that data. Every write commits by a round trip to a majority, so the cluster
  handles one serialised stream of decisions — order of thousands per second
  inside a datacentre — while session writes scale with users and arrive
  constantly. The dataset is expected to fit in memory on every node, because
  each node holds a full copy, so sessions would grow it without bound. And the
  blast radius changes: today losing a majority stops elections and
  configuration changes, which most systems survive for a few minutes; once
  sessions live there, the same event logs everybody out. Sessions want a
  partitioned key-value store with expiry, and the consensus cluster keeps the
  small, rarely written facts that must not disagree.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A leader-elected job holds a 30-second lease. Its process pauses for 45 seconds on a garbage collection, wakes up, and continues writing to shared storage. What has happened, and what would have prevented the damage?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Its lease expired while it was paused, the group elected a new leader, and the
  old one resumed with no idea that any time had passed — it had not crashed,
  and nothing tells a process it was frozen. Two leaders are now writing to the
  same storage, each correctly believing it holds the lease: split brain, from a
  pause rather than a network fault. Checking the lease before writing does not
  fix it, because the pause can land between the check and the write. What
  prevents the damage is fencing: each leadership term carries a number that
  only increases, the leader sends it with every write, and the storage rejects
  any write whose token is lower than the highest it has seen — so the old
  leader's writes are refused rather than merely discouraged. Where the storage
  cannot check a token, the remaining option is to make the writes idempotent
  and keyed so a stale leader cannot corrupt anything.
</PracticeCheck>
