## What you'll learn

- Write the requirements sheet for a Spotify-like music platform, exclusions included.
- Derive plays per second, concurrent streams, egress and event volume from stated assumptions.
- Name which of those numbers decides the architecture, and why the byte total is not it.

## Intuition

**A music platform is a metadata system with an audio attachment.** That
reframing is the whole module. The catalogue is enormous in rows and small in
bytes: a hundred million tracks, but a three-minute track at 128 kbps is about
3 MB, so the entire audio library is on the order of a petabyte — less than two
weeks of uploads on the video platform estimated in
[Back-of-Envelope Estimation](/learn/sd-capacity-estimation/). What is large here
is the number of things, and the number of times per hour a listener asks for one.

The second model follows from the first. **A listening session is a burst of
small requests, not one long byte stream.** A viewer starts a film and the system
does one authorisation, one manifest fetch and then thirty minutes of cached
segment reads. A listener starts a track and the same work happens again three
minutes later, twenty times a day, on every device they own. Sessions that touch
dozens of objects are what make this platform's shape different from a streaming
platform's: a viewer takes half an hour of media in one session, and a listener
takes an hour of it in twenty.

Three systems hide behind "play a song", and it is worth separating them before
any box is drawn: the **catalogue** (read-mostly, cacheable, shared by everyone),
**personal state** (playlists, likes, history — written constantly, private, and
the only place a consistency argument happens), and the **audio bytes**
(immutable, large, and never touched by an application server). Each of the three
has a different read-to-write ratio, a different consistency requirement and a
different store.

## How it works

Start with the sheet, in the shape
[Functional and Non-Functional Requirements](/learn/sd-requirements/) uses. Every
figure below is **a teaching assumption** chosen to keep the example concrete,
and the listener figures are the ones that lesson already assumed, so the two
agree.

```text
Music platform — requirements (Spotify-like; every scale figure is an assumption)

Functional
  F1  A listener searches the catalogue by track, artist, album or playlist.
  F2  A listener plays a track, pauses, skips, and resumes on another device.
  F3  A listener creates playlists, adds and reorders tracks, follows others, and
      edits a playlist a collaborator may be editing at the same moment.
  F4  A listener likes a track or follows an artist, and browses that library.
  F5  The home screen is personalised from what this listener has played.
  F6  A listener downloads a playlist for offline playback while subscribed.
  F7  Every play emits a listening event, for personalisation and for royalties.
  F8  Podcast episodes are catalogue items with the same operations, plus a
      saved position per listener per episode.
  F9  A listener browses what they have recently played, per device and merged.

Non-functional
  N1  p99 under 250 ms from pressing play to the first audio byte.
  N2  99.9% availability for playback, 99.5% for writes to personal state.
  N3  An acknowledged playlist edit survives the loss of one machine.
  N4  An edit or a play is visible on the listener's other devices within 5 s.
  N5  Territory rights are checked when playback is authorised, not on the client.
  N6  Concurrent edits to one playlist merge rather than overwrite, under a rule
      stated per pair of operations.

Out of scope
  X1  Ingesting audio from labels and distributors.
  X2  Royalty accounting beyond emitting a correct, deduplicated event.
  X3  Live audio, social feeds and user-uploaded tracks.

Assumptions
  A1  100,000,000 tracks; 50,000,000 daily listeners.
  A2  20 plays per listener per day, 3 minutes each.
  A3  Delivered audio averages 128 kbps across the rendition ladder.
  A4  5 listening events per play: start, three heartbeats, end.
  A5  Podcast episodes number in the low millions, 40 minutes each at 64 kbps.
```

Now the arithmetic. A day rounds to 100,000 seconds and peak is roughly three
times average, exactly as the estimation lesson sets out.

```text
Play requests
  50,000,000 × 20           =  1,000,000,000 plays/day
  1,000,000,000 ÷ 100,000   =      10,000 plays/s average
  peak ≈ 3 × average        =      30,000 plays/s

Concurrent streams and egress
  1,000,000,000 × 180 s     =  180,000,000,000 listening-seconds/day
  ÷ 100,000                 =  1,800,000 concurrent streams
  1,800,000 × 128 kbps      ≈  230,000 Mbps  =  230 Gbps average
  peak ≈ 3 × average        ≈  700 Gbps

Listening events
  1,000,000,000 × 5         =  5,000,000,000 events/day
  ÷ 100,000                 =  50,000 events/s average, 150,000/s peak
  × 200 B per event         ≈  1 TB/day of raw events

Audio at rest
  180 s × (96 + 128 + 256) kbps = 86,400 kbit ≈ 10 MB per track, all renditions
  100,000,000 × 10 MB           ≈ 1 PB, before replication

Catalogue metadata
  100,000,000 × 2 kB            ≈ 200 GB for every track in the catalogue

Personal state
  50,000,000 × 20 playlists × 40 items = 40,000,000,000 playlist items
  × 50 B per item                      ≈ 2 TB, before replication
  2 playlist edits per listener/day    ≈ 1,000 writes/s, 3,000/s peak
  reads at 100 to 1                    ≈ 100,000 playlist reads/s
```

**Read the numbers, not the arithmetic.** Ten thousand plays per second is the
figure that shapes this design, because each one is an authorisation, a rights
check, a metadata read and a signed URL — a small pile of work that recurs every
three minutes per listener. Compare the video platform in the estimation lesson:
180,000 concurrent streams carrying 540 Gbps average — a tenth of the concurrency
here, moving more than twice the bytes, from 10,000,000 daily viewers who between
them start about a hundred sessions per second. Normalise the two by the media
they actually deliver and the shapes separate cleanly: per hour of media, the
video platform moves more than twenty times the bits (3 Mbps against 128 kbps)
and starts a tenth as many sessions (one per half-hour against one per three
minutes). Opposite bottlenecks, from the same play button.

Two hundred and thirty gigabits per second still needs a delivery network — no
origin fleet should answer that from one place — but it is not the deciding
number, and at these volumes the delivery network is a commodity purchase rather
than an architecture. The petabyte is more interesting than it looks: an entire
audio catalogue that fits in a petabyte is small enough that caches near
listeners can hold a large share of what anybody asks for, which is why a design
like this can assume a high cache hit ratio rather than engineering for a low one.

The two hundred gigabytes of metadata is the quiet result. **The whole catalogue
fits in memory across a modest cache tier**, so catalogue reads need never touch
a primary database in the request path. Almost nothing else in this course is
small enough for that to be true.

Personal state is the third shape, and its three figures point in three
directions. Forty billion playlist items is a partitioning decision — by owner,
so one listener's rows stay together. A hundred thousand playlist reads per
second is a caching decision, at a hundred reads per write. And a thousand writes
per second is small, comfortably one well-partitioned store. **The hard problem
in playlists is not volume; it is that two devices and a collaborator may write
the same list at once**, which is why it gets a lesson of its own rather than a
capacity plan.

Fifty thousand events per second is the last one, and it rules out the obvious
implementation. That is not a row inserted per event into a relational primary;
it is an append to a partitioned log with several independent consumers, which is
[Event Streams](/learn/sd-event-streams/) and the subject of the last lesson in
this module.

<Callout variant="note">
  **Podcasts are the same objects with different constants.** An episode is 40
  minutes rather than 3, so an hour of podcast listening generates a fraction of
  the play requests an hour of music does; speech encodes acceptably at half the
  bitrate, so it moves fewer bytes; and because an episode is long, a saved
  position per listener becomes a requirement rather than a nicety. The
  recommendation signal is weaker too — an item with thousands of plays rather
  than millions has far less co-occurrence data to learn from.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Fixing the assumptions rather than ranging them.** Twenty plays a listener a
day is one number standing in for a distribution, and every figure below it
inherits that. Ranging each would be more honest and would produce a spread
nobody can design against; one round number produces 10,000 plays per second,
which is a decision. The cost is that a challenge to A2 moves everything at once.

**Averages first, peaks only where they bind.** A peak factor is applied to the
two figures that size a system — events and playlist writes — and nowhere else.
It buys arithmetic short enough to say out loud, and costs a reader who takes
230 Gbps for a provisioning target rather than a mean.

**A catalogue small enough to cache is a licensed catalogue.** The 200 GB of
metadata fits in a cache tier because a hundred million tracks arrive through
rights deals rather than an upload button; the
[YouTube-like platform](/learn/sd-youtube-requirements/) buys an unbounded
catalogue and pays in storage its users decide.

## Common pitfalls

- **Sizing this like a video platform.** Bandwidth is the reflex answer for
  anything with a play button, and here it is the least constraining number on
  the sheet. Design for the request rate and the event rate.
- **Treating the catalogue as one thing.** Track metadata, audio bytes and rights
  have different sizes, different change rates and different consistency needs;
  a single "catalogue service" that owns all three inherits the strictest of each.
- **Forgetting that rights are a requirement, not a detail.** N5 says a rights
  check happens on every authorisation. That single line rules out handing
  clients long-lived audio URLs, and it decides the delivery design two lessons
  from now.
- **Assuming listening events are analytics.** They pay artists. An event
  pipeline that may silently drop 0.1% is fine for a dashboard and not fine for
  a royalty statement, and knowing that at requirements time changes what gets built.

## Interview notes

Open by naming the bottleneck rather than the components. "A three-minute item
restarts the authorisation, the rights check and the metadata read twenty times a
day per listener, so 10,000 plays per second is the shaping number and 230 Gbps
of egress is a purchase" reframes a question most candidates answer with
bandwidth because it has a play button.

Then volunteer the 200 GB. A catalogue whose metadata fits entirely in a cache
tier — which almost nothing else at this scale does — makes caching a conclusion
drawn from the estimate rather than the reflex "we'd put a cache in front of the
database".

## Key takeaways

- The catalogue is **large in rows and small in bytes**: 100,000,000 tracks,
  roughly 1 PB of audio and around 200 GB of metadata.
- **Request rate, not bandwidth, is the shaping number** — about 10,000 plays per
  second average and 30,000 at peak, because a three-minute item restarts the
  work twenty times a day per listener.
- Catalogue metadata is small enough to **cache in its entirety**, which almost
  nothing else at this scale is.
- **50,000 listening events per second** is an append-only log with several
  consumers, not a database insert per event.
- Every figure here is **a stated assumption**; changing one changes the fleet
  size, and the structural conclusions survive.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. Per hour of media delivered, this platform starts twenty sessions where the video platform in Back-of-Envelope Estimation starts two, and moves a twentieth of the bits. Why does that make one design worry about request rate and the other about bandwidth?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Because item length and bitrate pull in opposite directions. An hour of music
  is twenty separate items, each needing its own authorisation, rights check,
  metadata read and URL — so an hour of listening produces twenty session
  starts, while an hour of viewing produces one or two. That is what puts play
  requests at 10,000 per second here. Bitrate does the reverse: 128 kbps against
  3 Mbps is more than a twenty-fold difference per second of media, so an hour
  of viewing costs more than twenty times the bytes an hour of listening does.
  The two platform totals are not directly comparable — 230 Gbps here carries
  50,000,000 listener-hours a day, 540 Gbps there carries 5,000,000 viewer-hours
  — and it is dividing each by its media-hours that recovers the twenty-fold
  ratio. Short items make request rate the constraint; large items make bytes
  the constraint.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. Which of the estimates change if podcasts become a significant share of listening, and which do not?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Play requests fall per hour of listening, because a 40-minute episode replaces
  roughly thirteen three-minute tracks — the same listening hour generates far
  fewer authorisations. Egress falls a little too, since speech is acceptable at
  a lower bitrate than music. Listening events per hour fall for the same reason
  as play requests, though a saved position per episode adds a small, steady
  write that music did not have. Catalogue metadata barely moves: A5 puts
  podcast episodes in the low millions against a hundred million tracks. Audio
  at rest grows, because a 40-minute episode is several times more bytes than a
  track even at half the bitrate.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A reviewer challenges assumption A2 and says listeners average 40 plays a day, not 20. What has to be recalculated, and which conclusions survive?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Everything driven by listening doubles: about 20,000 plays per second average
  and 60,000 at peak, roughly 3,600,000 concurrent streams, around 460 Gbps
  average egress, and 100,000 listening events per second. Everything driven by
  the catalogue is untouched — a petabyte of audio and 200 GB of metadata depend
  on how many tracks exist, not on how often anyone plays them. That split is
  the point: the rate figures size today's fleet, and doubling them is a
  purchasing decision. The structural conclusions all survive, because they came
  from ratios rather than absolutes: still a delivery network, still a cacheable
  catalogue, still a log rather than a table for events.
</PracticeCheck>
