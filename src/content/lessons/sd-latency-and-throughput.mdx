## What you'll learn

- Read a latency distribution and say what p50, p95 and p99 each tell you.
- Predict the point where rising utilisation turns into rising latency.
- Convert between throughput, latency and concurrency, and size a pool from it.
- State what "scalable" means as a measurable claim rather than a compliment.

## Intuition

Two numbers describe any request path, and they answer different questions.
**Latency** is how long one operation takes, measured end to end from the
caller's point of view. **Throughput** is how much work completes per unit of
time — for a request path, QPS. A system can have excellent throughput and
terrible latency, and the reverse; improving one often damages the other.

**Latency is a distribution, not a number.** Every request in a window has its
own duration, and the useful summary is a percentile: p50 is the value half of
requests come in at or below, p99 the value 99% come in at or below. An average
collapses that distribution into a single figure that may describe no request
that actually happened.

The mental model for throughput is **a counter with a queue**. The server is the
person at the counter; service time is how long one customer takes; utilisation
is the fraction of the time the counter is busy. Throughput is customers served
per minute — but the customer's experience is service time _plus_ the wait, and
the wait is governed by how busy the counter already is.

## How it works

Start with why the average misleads. Take a thousand requests where nearly all
are fast and a few are not:

```text
1,000 requests: 980 at 40 ms, 20 at 4,000 ms
mean = (980 × 40 + 20 × 4,000) ÷ 1,000 = 119 ms
p50  = 40 ms       (the median request)
p99  = 4,000 ms    (2% were slow, so the 99th percentile sits in the slow group)
```

The mean of 119 ms is a duration no request took: slower than the typical
experience, and more than thirty times faster than the worst. Both halves of that
sentence are reasons not to alert on it.

The tail matters more than its share suggests, because pages are assembled from
many calls. Say a page needs 20 backend responses and each call is slow 1% of
the time:

```text
20 calls per page, each slow 1% of the time
all 20 fast = 0.99^20 ≈ 0.82
so ≈ 18 page loads in 100 contain at least one slow call
```

A 1% tail in one service became an 18% tail on the page. This is why fan-out
architectures are judged on p99 and p99.9: the service's rare case is the page's
common case. The standard name for the effect is tail latency, from Dean and
Barroso's 2013 article _The Tail at Scale_.

Where does the tail come from? Mostly from queueing. For a single server with
randomly arriving work, waiting time grows with utilisation like this:

```text
service time 10 ms, one server, random arrivals
50% busy → wait ≈ 10 × 0.5 ÷ 0.5   =  10 ms   (total ≈  20 ms)
90% busy → wait ≈ 10 × 0.9 ÷ 0.1   =  90 ms   (total ≈ 100 ms)
99% busy → wait ≈ 10 × 0.99 ÷ 0.01 = 990 ms   (total ≈ 1,000 ms)
```

That formula is the textbook single-server queue — a teaching model, not a
description of any real service, whose burstier arrivals make the effect worse
rather than better. The lesson survives the simplification: **the last slice of
capacity costs almost all of the latency.** Going from 50% to 90% busy
multiplies the response time by five; 90% to 99% multiplies it by ten again.
Utilisation is not a number to maximise.

The third piece of arithmetic ties the two properties together. Concurrency —
the number of requests in flight at once — equals throughput multiplied by
latency. This is Little's Law, and it is the fastest way to size a pool:

```text
2,000 requests/second × 50 ms  = 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 requests in flight
2,000 requests/second × 250 ms = 2,000 × 0.25 = 500 requests in flight
```

A connection pool of 200 is comfortable in the first line and a wall in the
second. Nothing about the traffic changed; a dependency got five times slower
and the pool you sized for the fast case became the bottleneck. Most capacity
surprises are this calculation, run too late.

Finally, budget the latency you have promised, hop by hop:

```text
p99 budget 300 ms for one page
  network to the edge, TLS        60 ms
  gateway and authentication      20 ms
  service fan-out, slowest call  120 ms
  serialisation and render        40 ms
  headroom                        60 ms
```

The split is a teaching assumption; the discipline is general. Every hop gets a
number a named team owns, and headroom is a line item rather than an accident.

<Callout variant="note">
  **Scalability, stated as a claim you can check.** A system is scalable when
  adding resources raises throughput roughly in proportion while latency stays
  inside its budget. Scaling *vertically* — a bigger machine — is the simplest
  change available and has a ceiling plus a single failure domain. Scaling
  *horizontally* — more machines — has no ceiling in principle, but only if the
  work can be split and the shared state behind it does not become the new
  bottleneck. Note what horizontal scaling does not do: ten servers serve ten
  times the requests, and each request still takes exactly as long.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Throughput and latency pull against each other.** Batching, compression and
larger queues all raise throughput by making individual operations wait longer.
A batch window of 20 ms is 20 ms added to every request in it, bought in
exchange for fewer round trips.

**Each further percentile costs more than the last.** p99.9 is the rarest one
request in a thousand, and it is usually made of garbage collection pauses, cold
caches, a retried packet or a node that is about to fail. Squeezing it means
duplicating work or over-provisioning.

**Flat latency is bought with idle capacity.** The queueing table above is also a
price list: running at 50% utilisation means paying for roughly twice the
hardware in use at any instant. That is what a stable p99 costs, and it is a
budget decision as much as an engineering one.

## Common pitfalls

- **Alerting on the mean.** Tempting because every tool offers it as the default
  and it is one number. It hides exactly the requests users complain about.
- **Averaging percentiles.** The mean of ten servers' p99 values is not the p99
  of the fleet, and yesterday's p99 averaged with today's is not this week's.
  Dashboards will do this happily; percentiles must be computed from the
  underlying distribution.
- **Measuring inside the server.** A timer started when the handler begins
  excludes the time the request spent in the accept queue — which is the part
  that grows first under load. Measure at the client, or at the edge.
- **Coordinated omission in load tests.** A generator that waits for each
  response before sending the next one stops sending during a stall, so the
  stall is missing from its own results — the tool reports health during the
  outage it caused.
- **Sizing pools, threads and timeouts for the fast case.** Little's Law says
  the in-flight count moves with latency, so every pool is implicitly sized for
  a latency you have assumed rather than promised.

## Interview notes

Say targets with three parts: a percentile, a scope and a window. "p99 under
300 ms for the feed read path, measured at the client, over five minutes" is a
requirement someone can test. "Low latency" is not.

The other habit that reads well is separating the two words on purpose. When
someone says "make it faster", ask whether each request should finish sooner or
the system should handle more of them — the fixes point in opposite directions,
caching and fewer hops for the first, batching and more workers for the second.

## Key takeaways

- Latency is a **distribution**; report percentiles, and never the mean alone.
- A small tail becomes a large one under fan-out: **20 calls at 1% slow each
  make roughly 18% of pages slow.**
- Waiting time grows with utilisation, steeply near the top. **Running hot is
  paid for in p99**, not in throughput.
- **Concurrency = throughput × latency.** Every pool size is a bet on a latency.
- Horizontal scaling raises throughput; it does not make a single request
  faster.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A dashboard shows average response time 119 ms, and users report the site "sometimes hangs for seconds". Both can be true — how?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Because the mean is a summary of a distribution with a long tail. In the
  worked example, 980 of 1,000 requests took 40 ms and 20 took 4 seconds; the
  mean of 119 ms describes neither group, and the 2% who waited four seconds are
  the people filing the reports. The p50 (40 ms) shows the typical experience
  and the p99 (4,000 ms) shows the complaint. The mean cannot tell you *which*
  requests moved — the same 119 ms would come from 2% of users waiting four
  seconds or from every user being slightly slower — and those two situations
  need completely different work, which is why a mean is not a number to alert
  on.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A service runs at 85% utilisation. A new feature adds 10% to the work each request does. Why is the latency risk much larger than 10%?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Because utilisation rises with the work, and waiting time rises far faster
  than utilisation does. At 85% busy the queueing multiplier is 0.85 ÷ 0.15 ≈
  5.7 service times of waiting. Adding 10% to each request pushes utilisation to
  about 93.5%, where the multiplier is 0.935 ÷ 0.065 ≈ 14.4 — and each of those
  service times is now 10% longer. The wait roughly triples for a 10% change in
  work. This is the shape of most "it was fine until suddenly it wasn't"
  incidents: the system was already on the steep part of the curve.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A service handles 2,000 requests per second with a 200-connection pool to its database, where calls average 50 ms. The database slows to 250 ms. What happens, and why is it not a 5× slowdown?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  By Little's Law the service needs throughput × latency = 2,000 × 0.25 = 500
  connections in flight to sustain the same rate, and it has 200. The pool
  therefore caps throughput at 200 ÷ 0.25 = 800 requests per second, so 1,200
  requests per second arrive with nowhere to go. They queue in front of the
  pool, and waiting time grows without bound while arrivals exceed service — so
  latency does not settle at 250 ms, or even at 1,250 ms; it climbs until
  something times out or sheds load. A 5× dependency slowdown became an outage
  because a fixed pool turned it into an overload.
</PracticeCheck>
