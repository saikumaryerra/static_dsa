## What you'll learn

- Say what a user-upload catalogue changes about caching, and what it leaves unchanged.
- Size a cache hierarchy from a hit ratio and an egress figure.
- Handle a cold video that becomes hot in minutes without collapsing the origin.
- Place live streaming beside video on demand, and say when live is the wrong tool.

## Intuition

The delivery mechanism itself is not new here. Manifests, fixed-length segments,
adaptive bitrate and encrypted-once media are covered in
[video delivery](/learn/sd-netflix-video-delivery/), and every word of it applies
unchanged: a stream is a playlist of small immutable files that the client picks
one at a time, and no application server is on the byte path.

**What changes is how the caches get filled.** A curated platform knows next
week's popular titles and pushes copies to caches during off-peak hours. Here
nobody knows — a video uploaded four minutes ago may be about to take a
measurable share of global egress, or be watched twice ever, and no signal
available at publish time separates them reliably. So the fill is
**pull-through**: the first viewer's request misses every cache, travels to
origin, and populates the tiers on its way back.

The second model follows from that. **A cache is a popularity filter, and the
tail passes straight through it.** Evicting least-recently-used over a request
stream where most videos are asked for once means tail videos never survive long
enough to be hit again. That is the cache working correctly — you do not want a
petabyte of once-watched video occupying edge disks — but it means the tail's
requests are origin traffic by design, and origin has to be sized for that rather
than treated as a rare fallback.

The third: **hot is a rate of change, not a level.** The dangerous case is not the
video with a billion lifetime views, which every cache holds. It is the one that
goes from zero to millions in twenty minutes, because for the first few seconds
every cache in the world misses the same key simultaneously.

## How it works

```text
player
  |
  '-> metadata service: video record, manifest URL, playback token
  |
  '-> manifest: the rungs that exist right now (short TTL)
  |
  '-> segment GET
        |
        '-> edge cache -- miss -> regional cache -- miss -> origin storage
```

**1. Starting playback.** A view start is a metadata read plus a manifest fetch
plus a counter increment, and at 40,000 starts per second average — 120,000 at
peak — it is an ordinary high-volume web path. One detail differs from a curated
platform: the manifest lists the renditions that exist **now**, which for a
recently published video may be a single modest rung while the rest of the ladder
is still encoding. The manifest therefore has a short time to live, or a version
in its URL that changes when a rung is added, whereas the segments themselves are
immutable and can be cached for as long as anyone likes.

**2. The object count.** A ten-minute video at four-second segments across the
module's six-rung ladder is roughly nine hundred objects, and there are a billion
videos. That puts
the catalogue at the order of a trillion cacheable objects — which matters
because a cache node's index has to address them. Object count, and not only byte
volume, is what sizes a cache tier.

**3. The hierarchy.** Three tiers, filled on demand — the edge, shield and origin
tiers of any [delivery network](/learn/sd-cdn/):

```text
Cache arithmetic (teaching assumptions)
  top 1,000,000 videos take ~90% of views, ~1 GB of ladder each
    hot working set                                =    1 PB
    whole catalogue                                =    1 EB
  edge tier sized to hold the hot set              -> ~90% hit ratio
  misses at peak: 10% of 100 Tbps                  =   10 Tbps upstream
  10 Tbps ÷ 100 Gbps per interface                 =  100 saturated links
```

Ten terabits per second is why there is a middle tier. A regional cache is
large enough to hold the near-tail — content too cold for an edge and far too
warm to fetch from storage every time — and catching most of that remainder
leaves origin serving a few terabits per second, which object storage absorbs.
The edge answers the head, the regional tier answers the shoulder, and origin
answers the tail.

**4. Caches inside networks.** Netflix's Open Connect programme is the
_publicly documented_ example of placing caching appliances inside internet
service providers' own networks, and doing so is _generally accepted industry
architecture_ for any platform at this egress. The difference here is what fills
them: Open Connect can preposition during off-peak because a curated catalogue's
popularity is predictable, while an upload platform's embedded caches fill from
live requests and earn their value on the head alone.

**5. When a cold video becomes hot.** The first thousand players ask for the same
segment inside a second, every cache misses, and each miss becomes an origin
request — a stampede against a key that will be trivially cacheable ten seconds
later. Request coalescing — the [delivery network](/learn/sd-cdn/) defence
against a thundering herd — is the first answer: one fetch per key proceeds while
the others wait on it. What differs here is that the key has never been requested
at all, so the herd hits every tier cold at once rather than at a TTL expiry, and
coalescing must work at the regional tier too. The second mechanism, **prewarming
on a signal**, uses what the platform does know: an upload from a
channel with millions of subscribers, or a video whose view rate crosses a
threshold, is worth pushing to regional caches before demand arrives. Both are
_generally accepted_ practice; neither removes the need for the other.

<Callout variant="note">
  **Live streaming, at concept level.** Live inverts three assumptions. The
  encoder is the origin, so segments appear a few seconds before anyone can
  request them and nothing can be prepositioned. The manifest is rewritten
  continuously as a sliding window rather than fetched once. And segment length
  becomes the dominant latency term: shorter segments cut glass-to-glass delay
  and multiply request rate and manifest churn. Live is the wrong tool for
  anything not actually happening now — a premiere of a finished video is
  cheaper, more cacheable and more reliable as ordinary on-demand playback with
  a scheduled publish.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Pull-through against prepositioning.** Pull-through needs no prediction and
wastes nothing on videos nobody watches; it pays for that with a cold-start miss
for every first viewer and with stampedes on sudden popularity. Prepositioning
inverts both, and is only available where popularity can be predicted.

**Ladder depth on the tail.** Cutting tail videos to two or three rungs saves
storage and concentrates their cache footprint, at the price of a worse network
match for the few viewers they get — a trade the [scale
lesson](/learn/sd-youtube-scale/) takes further.

**A regional tier against direct origin reads.** The middle tier is real hardware
that exists to serve content nobody would call popular. Removing it saves that
cost and moves the entire miss stream onto origin, whose latency and per-request
cost are both far higher.

## Common pitfalls

- **Believing a delivery network fixes the hit ratio.** Hit ratio is a property
  of the request distribution, not of the vendor: over a long-tail catalogue a
  cache holds the head and no purchase changes that.
- **A single flat cache tier.** It looks simpler and it forces every non-head
  request onto origin, which is the ten-terabit number above.
- **No request coalescing.** Invisible until the first viral video, then it
  arrives as an origin outage that looks like a traffic spike.
- **Caching the manifest as aggressively as the segments.** They look like the
  same kind of object and are not — one changes when a rendition is added, the
  other never changes at all.
- **Reporting one hit ratio for the whole system.** A healthy overall number
  hides the tier that is missing badly, and the tiers have completely different
  jobs.

## Interview notes

The sentence that shows you have thought about this rather than read about CDNs
is the contrast: "the delivery mechanism is the same as any streaming platform,
but the cache-fill story is the opposite, because we cannot know what will be
popular." Everything else in the lesson follows from it and can be derived on the
spot.

Then reach for the arithmetic rather than an adjective. "A ninety per cent edge
hit ratio still leaves ten terabits per second going upstream at peak, so there
has to be a middle tier" is an argument; "we'd add a CDN" is a preference.

## Key takeaways

- The delivery mechanism is unchanged from a curated platform; **the cache-fill
  strategy is inverted** — pull-through, not prepositioned.
- Caches hold the head by design, so **the tail's requests are origin traffic**
  and origin must be sized for them.
- A **tiered hierarchy** exists because the miss stream from the edge is itself
  a very large egress figure.
- Sudden popularity is handled by **request coalescing** at the caches and
  **prewarming on a signal**, not by hoping the edge fills in time.
- **Live streaming makes prepositioning impossible by definition** and makes
  segment length the dominant latency term.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A colleague proposes copying the curated platform's approach: push every new video to edge caches during off-peak hours so the first viewer never misses. What is wrong with it here?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  The arithmetic kills it before the design questions do. Three million uploads
  a day at about a gigabyte of ladder each is roughly three petabytes of new
  content daily, against an edge tier sized at around a petabyte in total — the
  push would overwrite the entire hot set several times over every day and evict
  precisely the content that was serving 90% of views. The deeper problem is
  that the strategy assumes a signal that does not exist: prepositioning is
  worthwhile when you can predict which titles will be watched, and here the
  great majority of those three million videos will be watched a handful of
  times ever. What survives from the idea is the narrow version — prewarming
  regional caches for the few uploads that carry a real predictor, such as a
  channel with millions of subscribers — which the design already uses.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A video goes from unknown to a million concurrent viewers in twenty minutes. Trace what happens at each tier, and name the mechanism that keeps origin standing.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  At the first burst every edge cache misses the same segment key at the same
  moment, because the object has never been requested. Each miss goes to the
  regional tier, which also misses, and each of those becomes an origin read for
  one object — so the request amplification is roughly the number of edge nodes
  seeing traffic, all for a file that would be a hit ten seconds later. Request
  coalescing is the mechanism that saves it: each cache node allows one fetch
  per key to proceed and parks the rest of the requests on that single in-flight
  fetch, so a thousand simultaneous misses become one upstream read and the
  amplification collapses to one per tier per node. After the first few seconds
  the situation reverses completely — the video is now the most popular object
  in every cache, the hit ratio is near perfect, and the load returns to being
  pure edge egress.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. To save money, someone proposes deleting the regional cache tier: tail videos are watched so rarely that their requests may as well go straight to origin storage.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  The premise is right about the tail and wrong about what the tier is holding.
  The regional cache is not there for the coldest content; it is there for the
  shoulder — videos too cold to survive eviction at an edge and warm enough to
  be requested repeatedly across a region. That band is where the edge's misses
  actually come from, and it is a large fraction of them: on the numbers in this
  lesson a 90% edge hit ratio leaves ten terabits per second at peak going
  upstream, and sending all of it to object storage means both an enormous read
  rate against a store priced per request and a much longer path for every one
  of those viewers. The honest version of the proposal is to shrink the tier and
  measure the resulting miss stream, not to remove it — and if it is removed,
  the saving has to be weighed against origin capacity that now has to carry a
  peak it was never sized for.
</PracticeCheck>
