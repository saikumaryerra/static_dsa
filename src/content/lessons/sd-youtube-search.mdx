## What you'll learn

- Say what a video search engine actually indexes, and where that text comes from.
- Choose between document and term partitioning, and justify the choice with a query pattern.
- Serve a new upload in search results within minutes without rebuilding a billion-document index.
- Size autocomplete separately from search, and say when it is not worth building.

## Intuition

**Searching video is searching the text around video.** The media itself is
opaque to an index: what is matched on is the title, the description, the tags,
the channel name and — the field that does most of the work on long videos — the
transcript produced during the upload pipeline. A video search engine is
therefore an ordinary text search engine over a corpus that the ingest pipeline
generates, which is why everything in
[search indexes](/learn/sd-search-indexes/) transfers directly.

The second model is the one that resolves the freshness problem. **There are two
indexes, not one.** A large base index over the whole catalogue is expensive to
build and cheap to query; a small near-real-time index holds only the last few
hours of uploads and is cheap to update. Every query hits both and the results
are merged. Trying to make one structure both enormous and instantly mutable is
what makes freshness look impossible.

The third: **autocomplete is not search.** It matches prefixes against a set of
_queries people have typed_, not against the corpus, it runs at several times the
query rate because a single search produces several keystroke requests, and its
latency budget is tens of milliseconds. Different data, different rate, different
deadline — so a different system.

## How it works

**1. What gets indexed.** Fields carry different weights: an exact title match is
worth far more than the same words appearing somewhere in a transcript. Alongside
the matchable text sit fields that never match but always rank — view count,
average watch time per impression, channel history, upload date. Keeping those
two categories distinct in your head is what stops "add engagement to the index"
becoming a confused sentence.

**2. Partitioning.** Split by document: each shard holds a slice of the videos
and a complete inverted index over that slice. A query goes to every shard, each
returns its own top results, and a coordinator merges them.

```text
Index sizing (teaching assumptions)
  1,000,000,000 videos × ~1 KB of indexed text  =        1 TB of text
  inverted index with positions, ~2 ×           =        2 TB
  shards of ~100 GB                             =       20 shards
  × 3 replicas                                  =       60 index servers
  queries                                       =   10,000/s average
  peak ≈ 3 × average                            =   30,000/s peak
  every query touches every shard
  30,000 × 20                                   =  600,000 shard queries/s
```

The alternative, term partitioning, puts all postings for a given term on one
shard. A single-term query then touches one shard instead of twenty, which sounds
better until you notice two things: a common term makes its shard permanently
hot, and a multi-word query has to intersect posting lists that live on different
machines, moving large lists across the network mid-query. Document partitioning
spreads load evenly and keeps every intersection local, which is _generally
accepted industry architecture_ for corpora of this shape. Term partitioning
earns its place where queries are overwhelmingly single-term and the corpus is
small enough that a hot shard is not a problem.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **Fan-out makes the slowest shard your latency.** A query that waits for
  twenty shards has a p99 set by the worst of twenty responses, so ordinary
  per-server variability becomes systematic user-visible slowness — the effect
  Dean and Barroso named in _The Tail at Scale_ (CACM, 2013). The usual answers
  are hedged requests to a second replica after a short delay, and returning the
  merge when enough shards have answered rather than all of them.
</Callout>

**3. Ranking, in two phases.** Each shard does cheap retrieval first: text
matching plus a static quality score, producing a few thousand candidates it can
compute without a model. The coordinator then re-ranks the merged set with an
expensive model that can afford many features per document — relevance, watch
time per impression, channel history, recency, and light personalisation from the
viewer's recent activity. Heavy personalisation belongs to
[recommendations](/learn/sd-youtube-recommendations/), which has a different
budget.

**4. Ranking is adversarial.** Because ranking reads engagement signals, and
because anyone may upload, the signals are attacked: keyword-stuffed
descriptions, titles unrelated to content, purchased views, thumbnails that
promise something the video does not contain. A quality layer that demotes
documents on abuse signals is therefore part of the ranking system rather than an
add-on — this is one of the sharpest differences from a curated catalogue, where
the metadata is supplied by the rights holder and nobody is trying to game it.

**5. Freshness.** The publish event that the upload pipeline emits writes the
video into a recent index held in memory, sized for hours rather than years, and
searchable within seconds of arriving. Queries fan out to both indexes and the
coordinator merges — a split that is _generally accepted industry architecture_
wherever a large index has to look fresh. When the base index is next merged, the
documents it absorbs are dropped from the recent tier. The cost is that two
indexes must rank consistently: a document should not jump position purely
because it crossed from one to the other.

**6. Autocomplete.** Build it from the query log, not the corpus. Count queries
over a rolling window, keep the top completions per prefix, and hold the result
in a compact in-memory structure served from a tier close to users.

```text
Autocomplete rate (teaching assumptions)
  4 prefix requests per search, after debouncing
  10,000 searches/s × 4     =   40,000 requests/s average
  peak ≈ 3 × average        =  120,000 requests/s peak
```

Four times the search rate against a data set measured in gigabytes is exactly
the shape that justifies a separate service. **When not to build one:** below a
few hundred queries per second, a prefix query against the main index is enough,
and the second system is cost with no benefit.

## Trade-offs

**Freshness against index efficiency.** A large immutable index is compact,
cache-friendly and fast; making it mutable costs all three. The two-index split
buys minutes-fresh results at the price of a second ranking path and a merge on
every query.

**Fan-out width against latency.** Fewer, larger shards mean fewer responses to
wait for and a worse tail per shard; more, smaller shards mean faster shards and
a wider fan-out. The tail-at-scale effect means the second is not free, and
hedged requests trade extra load for a better p99.

**Recall against precision.** Over a billion documents almost every query has
thousands of plausible matches, so finding documents is not the problem —
ordering them is. Effort spent widening retrieval usually pays less than effort
spent on ranking and on demoting abuse.

## Common pitfalls

- **Indexing metadata only.** Titles and descriptions are short and often
  inaccurate; transcripts are where a query about something said in minute
  fourteen can be answered.
- **One index for everything.** Tempting because it is one system, and it forces
  a choice between stale results and constantly rebuilding a billion documents.
- **Ranking on clicks.** The signal is abundant and it rewards misleading titles
  and thumbnails, because a click happens before the viewer knows they were
  misled.
- **Ignoring fan-out in the latency budget.** Each shard looks fast in isolation,
  and the query is as slow as the worst of twenty of them.
- **Autocomplete built from video titles.** It suggests strings nobody searches
  for, and misses the trending query that is three hours old.

## Interview notes

Two sentences carry most of the credit here. The first is what is indexed:
"searching video is searching titles, descriptions and transcripts, and the
transcripts come out of the ingest pipeline." The second is the two-index split
for freshness, which answers "how is a video findable a minute after upload?"
without anyone having to ask it.

If ranking comes up, say that it is adversarial before you list signals. On a
platform where anyone uploads, a candidate who names spam and view manipulation
as ranking inputs has understood something structural about user-generated
catalogues.

## Key takeaways

- A video search engine indexes **text about the video** — title, description,
  tags, channel, transcript — not the media.
- **Document partitioning** spreads load and keeps intersections local; term
  partitioning creates hot shards and cross-machine intersections.
- **Fan-out means the slowest shard sets p99** (Dean and Barroso, _The Tail at
  Scale_, 2013); hedged requests and partial merges are the standard answers.
- Freshness comes from a **second, small, in-memory index** merged at query time,
  not from mutating the base index.
- **Autocomplete is built from the query log**, runs at several times the search
  query rate, and is not worth a separate system at small scale.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. Why partition the index by document rather than by term, given that term partitioning would let a single-word query touch one shard instead of twenty?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Because the saving applies to the query shape that matters least and the costs
  apply to the one that matters most. Real search traffic is mostly multi-word,
  and under term partitioning a two-word query has to intersect two posting
  lists that live on different machines — which means shipping a potentially
  enormous list across the network inside the request, or a multi-round
  protocol, either way turning a local operation into a distributed one. Load
  distribution is the second problem: postings for a common word all sit on one
  shard, so that shard is hot for every query containing it while others idle,
  and no amount of replication fixes the skew cheaply. Document partitioning
  gives every shard a statistically similar slice of both the corpus and the
  traffic, and every intersection happens inside one machine. The price is the
  fan-out, which is real but is a latency problem with known mitigations rather
  than a load problem with none.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. The base index is rebuilt on a slow cycle, and the requirement is that a new upload is findable within minutes. How is that met, and what does it cost?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  With a second index. The publish event writes the video into a small
  near-real-time index that holds only recent uploads, lives in memory, and
  accepts writes continuously; every query is sent to both it and the base
  index, and the coordinator merges the two result sets. Because the recent
  index covers hours rather than years it stays small enough that mutability is
  affordable, and when the base index next absorbs those documents the recent
  tier drops them. The costs are real and worth stating: every query now pays a
  second fan-out and a merge, the two indexes must produce comparable scores or
  a document will visibly jump when it migrates, and there is a handover window
  where a document could appear in both and has to be deduplicated. What this
  buys is minutes of freshness over a billion documents without rebuilding
  anything.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A colleague proposes ranking primarily on click-through rate, arguing that it is the most abundant and least ambiguous signal the system has.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Abundant, yes; unambiguous, no — and on a platform where anyone uploads, the
  gap between those two is where the whole problem lives. A click is recorded
  before the viewer has seen any of the video, so it measures the title and the
  thumbnail rather than the content. Ranking on it therefore rewards whoever
  writes the most enticing promise, whether or not the video keeps it, and
  because ranking is a feedback loop the reward compounds: the misleading result
  ranks higher, collects more clicks, and ranks higher still. The fix is to move
  the objective past the click to something that only a satisfied viewer
  produces — watch time per impression, completion, or a return to the channel —
  which is the conclusion Covington, Adams and Sargin reached in the 2016 RecSys
  paper. Keep the click as one feature among many, add the abuse signals that
  catch purchased engagement, and never let the thing being optimised be an
  event that happens before the product is experienced.
</PracticeCheck>
