## What you'll learn

- Give two independent reasons no application server may stream video bytes.
- Follow a title from mezzanine master through encoding, packaging and caching.
- Explain adaptive bitrate, and say which party decides and why.
- Place DRM in the design without making it a delivery dependency.

## Intuition

**A stream is a playlist of small static files, chosen one at a time by the
client.** That single reframing is what makes internet video tractable, because
it turns a two-hour transmission into a few thousand ordinary cacheable GET
requests — and everything already known about caching reverse proxies
([content delivery networks](/learn/sd-cdn/)) applies unchanged.

The alternative — one long-lived connection per viewer, fed by a server — fails
for two independent reasons, and it is worth having both.

**Bandwidth.** The [requirements estimate](/learn/sd-netflix-requirements/) gives
about 90 Tbps at peak. At 100 Gbps per interface that is nine hundred saturated
links before redundancy, and buying them would still not help a viewer on another
continent, who pays roughly 150 ms of round trip whatever the origin's capacity.

**Connection lifetime.** A two-hour view holds a connection for 7,200 seconds. An
application tier is sized for requests measured in tens of milliseconds; it is
deployed daily, autoscaled hourly, and every one of those events would cut
viewing sessions. Segmenting converts 7.2 million long-lived sockets into short
independent requests that any cache can answer and any node can drop without a
viewer noticing.

## How it works

```text
mezzanine master (object storage)
  |
  '-> encode: a ladder of renditions, several codecs, audio tracks
        |
        '-> package: fixed-length segments, subtitles, manifest, encrypted once
              |
              '-> origin -> regional cache -> edge cache -> client
```

**1. The master.** Studios deliver a mezzanine file at a bitrate far above
anything a viewer receives. It lives in object storage, is never delivered, and is
the input every re-encode starts from — which is what makes adding a codec three
years later a batch job rather than a renegotiation.

**2. Encoding the ladder.** Each title is encoded into rungs from well under
1 Mbps up to the highest resolution offered, in more than one codec because a 2015
television and a current phone do not decode the same formats. With audio tracks
per language and subtitle files added, a title stores roughly fifty megabits per
second of content against the four megabits per second a viewer receives. Storing
twelve times what you deliver is affordable because the whole rendition set is
2 PB.

Because the catalogue is small and curated, encoding can be slow and careful:
tuning the ladder per title, so that a visually complex title gets more bits than
a flat animated one, is generally accepted industry practice wherever encode cost
is paid once and delivery cost is paid millions of times.

**3. Packaging.** The renditions are cut into fixed-length segments, typically a
few seconds each, and described by a manifest that lists every rung, every audio
track, every subtitle and the URLs of their segments. The industry has two
dominant manifest formats, HLS and DASH, and a player supports both. Segments are
encrypted once at packaging time, which is what lets them be cached as public
bytes.

**4. Adaptive bitrate.** Before each segment the player looks at its measured
throughput and its buffer occupancy and chooses which rung to fetch next. The
client decides because it is the only party that knows how much buffer it has,
how large its screen is and whether the network changed a second ago. Players
typically start on a low rung so playback begins quickly, then climb once the
buffer is comfortable — trading a few seconds of soft picture for a fast start.

**5. Caching near viewers.** Segments are immutable and shared by everyone
watching the title, which makes them close to ideal cache objects. Netflix's Open
Connect programme is the publicly documented version of taking this to its
conclusion: purpose-built caching appliances placed inside internet service
providers' own networks, so the bytes are served from inside the viewer's own
provider rather than from a distant origin. Open Connect's own documentation
describes the consequence: because it can predict with high accuracy what members
will watch and at what time of day, the vast majority of content updates are
downloaded to those appliances during configurable off-peak fill windows rather
than pulled on demand by the first viewer to ask. Prefilling is generally accepted
architecture wherever popularity can be forecast.

**6. DRM, at concept level.** The segments are encrypted; the keys are not in
them. The player asks a licence service for a key, the licence service checks that
this account is entitled to this title on this device, and it returns key material
bound to that session. Two rules follow: the licence request is on the control
plane and must never be cached by a shared cache, and the delivery network never
sees a key, which is exactly why it can serve the encrypted bytes to anyone.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **When this machinery is the wrong answer.** For clips of a few seconds,
  segmenting and manifests cost more than they save — serve one progressive
  file. For content that is free and unrestricted, DRM adds startup latency and
  a per-device support burden for nothing. And for small audio files, whole-file
  fetch with prefetch beats per-segment adaptation, which is why a music
  platform looks different here.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Ladder depth.** More rungs match more networks and cost storage, encode time and
cache efficiency: every rung is a separate object, so a deeper ladder spreads the
same demand over more cache entries and lowers the hit ratio on each.

**Codec choice.** A newer codec can carry the same quality in appreciably fewer
bytes, which is money at 90 Tbps, but costs more encode compute and excludes older
devices — so platforms ship several and let the manifest sort it out.

**Segment length.** Short segments adapt quickly and start fast; they also mean
more requests, more manifest entries and slightly worse compression. Long segments
compress better and react slowly, which a viewer feels as a stall when a network
degrades mid-segment.

**Client-side adaptation.** Putting the decision in the player is right because
the player has the information, and it means every client optimises for itself.
Several players sharing one home link can oscillate, each reading another's
backoff as spare capacity.

## Common pitfalls

- **Serving video through the application tier "for now".** Tempting during
  prototyping, and it embeds an assumption — that bytes flow through your servers —
  into clients, load balancers and monitoring, all of which have to be undone.
- **Caching a licence response.** It looks like any other JSON call, and a shared
  cache answering it means the entitlement check never runs — an account with no
  right to the title is handed a licence, and the key material inside it was bound
  to somebody else's session.
- **Purging the delivery network on every re-encode.** Versioned segment URLs make
  the new encode a new object, so old and new coexist and no invalidation is
  needed.
- **One ladder for the whole catalogue.** Convenient, and it overspends bits on
  simple content while starving complex content of them.
- **Measuring average delivered bitrate as the quality metric.** What viewers feel
  is time to first frame and rebuffer ratio; a high average bitrate with frequent
  stalls is a worse experience than a steady lower one.
- **Pricing only the video.** Ten audio tracks and twenty subtitle languages
  multiply manifest size, packaging work and storage alike.

## Interview notes

Say the sentence "no application server is on the byte path" and then give both
numbers — the interface arithmetic and the connection lifetime. Candidates who
give only bandwidth have usually read about CDNs; candidates who mention that a
two-hour socket cannot live on a fleet that redeploys daily have thought about
operating one.

The second thing to volunteer is where DRM sits. Placing the licence on the
control plane and the encrypted segments on the data plane, in one sentence, shows
the security model and the caching model were designed together rather than bolted
to each other.

## Key takeaways

- Segmented HTTP delivery turns a stream into **thousands of small immutable
  files**, which is what makes it cacheable at all.
- No application server is on the byte path for **two independent reasons**:
  aggregate bandwidth, and connection lifetime against a fleet that redeploys.
- **The client picks the rendition** for each segment, because only the client
  knows its buffer, its screen and its current network.
- Encode cost is paid **once per title**; delivery cost is paid every view, which
  is what justifies careful per-title encoding for a small catalogue.
- **Encrypt segments once, issue licences on the control plane**: the delivery
  network can then serve the bytes to anyone, and never holds a key.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A colleague suggests keeping one long-lived connection per viewer, served from a fleet of streaming servers, and points out that the fleet could be scaled horizontally. What are the two independent objections?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Horizontal scaling answers neither. The first objection is aggregate bandwidth
  and distance: about 90 Tbps at peak is roughly nine hundred saturated 100 Gbps
  interfaces, and even if the fleet owned them, a viewer on another continent
  still pays around 150 ms per round trip, which capacity cannot buy away. The
  second is independent of scale entirely: a connection that lives for two hours
  cannot sit on a fleet that is deployed daily and autoscaled hourly, because
  every deploy, every scale-in and every node failure cuts viewing sessions.
  Segmented delivery fixes both — the bytes come from caches near viewers, and
  each request is short enough that losing a node is invisible.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. The ladder has eight rungs and the team proposes four more for finer adaptation. What does that cost, beyond encoding time?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Storage grows by roughly the bitrate of the added rungs, which is real but the
  smallest cost here. The one people miss is cache efficiency: every rung is a
  distinct set of objects, so the same demand for a title now spreads across
  twelve sets instead of eight, each individually less popular. Cache footprint
  per title rises and the hit ratio on any given object falls, which at this
  scale is origin traffic rather than an inconvenience. Against that, the
  benefit is marginal — a player switching between two adjacent rungs that
  differ by a few hundred kilobits per second gains little quality and risks
  visible switching. Add rungs where the gaps are wide, particularly at the low
  end where networks are worst, not uniformly.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. The DRM licence request adds 120 ms to playback start, and someone proposes caching licence responses at the edge, keyed by title, to remove it.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  It would remove the latency and break the security model in the same change.
  Keyed by title in a shared cache, the licence service stops being consulted at
  all after the first request, so the entitlement check goes with it: an account
  with no right to the title is served a licence. The cached object itself then
  fails one of two ways. A licence carries key material bound to one account,
  device and session, so every later requester's decryption module rejects it
  and playback breaks for everyone but the first viewer; and if the binding is
  weak enough that it does not, what the cache is handing out is a content key
  that has escaped the account it was issued to. That is the "never cache a
  per-user response" rule in its most expensive form. Take the 120 ms off
  elsewhere: request the licence in parallel with the manifest rather than after
  it, keep the licence service in-region and reachable over a warm connection so
  the request costs one round trip instead of a handshake plus one, and start
  fetching the first low-rung segment while the licence is in flight. The
  encrypted segments are still fully cacheable; it is only the key that is not.
</PracticeCheck>
