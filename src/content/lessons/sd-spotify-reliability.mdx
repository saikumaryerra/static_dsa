## What you'll learn

- Design a listening-event pipeline for three consumers with three different guarantees.
- Absorb a scheduled burst without scaling the whole platform for it.
- Write the degradation ladder for a regional outage, in the order features are given up.

## Intuition

**One log, three durability requirements.** That is what is new here. Royalty
accounting must not lose or double-count an event, because it turns into money.
Personalisation can lose a fraction of a percent and nobody will ever detect it.
Analytics can be hours behind. Three pipelines for three guarantees is the
obvious mistake: it triples the client work, and the three then disagree about
what happened. One durable log that every consumer reads at its own pace, with
the strict guarantee in the strict consumer, is **generally accepted industry
architecture** for exactly this mismatch, and every figure below is **a teaching
assumption** carried from
[Requirements and Scale](/learn/sd-spotify-requirements/).

The second model is a callback.
[Scale, Reliability and Degradation](/learn/sd-netflix-reliability/) already
argued that degradation is designed rather than discovered; **the ranking is a
feature list written before the outage, not a decision taken during one.** Here
one thing dominates — audio that is already playing should keep playing — and
everything else, a shelf, a search result, a saved edit, can wait, be stale, or
be queued. What the log adds is a bottom rung that ladder did not need.

## How it works

**Events from the client.** A client buffers events and flushes at a track
change, so a play's five events travel in one request: 50,000 events per second
becomes about 10,000 requests per second. A timer alone would not do it — five
events across a three-minute play are already forty seconds apart, so a
thirty-second window flushes one event at a time. The buffer doubles as the
outbox that survives a tunnel. Delivery is at-least-once with jittered backoff,
so duplicates are expected rather than exceptional, and every event carries a
client-generated id that lets each consumer deduplicate;
[Idempotency, Retries and Backpressure](/learn/sd-idempotency-and-backpressure/)
is the mechanism that lets the pipeline be at-least-once and the royalty ledger
still correct.

**The log.** Events append to a partitioned log keyed by listener id.
Partitioning by listener gives ordering where ordering means something — one
listener's start, heartbeats and end stay in sequence — and parallelism
everywhere else — a shard key taken from the access pattern, as
[Replication, Partitioning and Sharding](/learn/sd-replication-and-sharding/)
advises. Consumers hold their own offsets, so a slow one falls behind without
slowing anybody else, and a fixed one can rewind and reprocess a week.

| Consumer            | Guarantee it needs       | How it gets it                    | Acceptable lag |
| ------------------- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Royalty ledger      | No loss, no double count | Dedupe on event id, replay on fix | Hours          |
| Personalisation     | Approximately complete   | Best effort, skip on error        | Minutes        |
| Analytics           | Complete eventually      | Batch load, reprocess nightly     | Hours          |
| Operational metrics | Fresh, not complete      | Sampled, aggregated in flight     | Seconds        |

**Retention is a reliability feature.** A log that keeps seven days lets a
consumer be broken for a day and catch up, and a bug fixed on Thursday be applied
to Monday's events. It is the cheapest disaster recovery in the design, and
[Event Streams](/learn/sd-event-streams/) explains why a queue, whose messages
are gone once consumed, cannot offer it.

**Bursts arrive on a schedule.** A major album releases at midnight local time; a
playlist push reaches millions of devices at once; a live event ends and everyone
opens the app. Assume a ten-fold spike lasting minutes, absorbed three ways. The
log takes the write side, because appending is cheap and consumers may lag ten
minutes without anybody noticing. The read side leans on caches, where the fully
cached catalogue pays off — a new release is one hot key, and request coalescing
keeps a million simultaneous misses from becoming a million origin reads. And the
client jitters anything scheduled, because ten million devices refreshing at
exactly midnight is a self-inflicted denial of service.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **Retry storms turn a blip into an outage.** A dependency slows, every client
  retries, the load doubles, more requests time out, and the system cannot
  recover even after the original cause is gone. Bounded retries, exponential
  backoff with jitter, and a circuit breaker that fails fast are what stop that.
</Callout>

**Regional outage.** Split the platform by what the data is. Catalogue and audio
are read-mostly and immutable, replicated everywhere and served locally, so a
region loss costs nothing there. Personal state has a home region per listener,
and losing it forces the choice
[Consistency Models, CAP and PACELC](/learn/sd-consistency-models/) describes:
read from a replica and refuse writes, or accept writes elsewhere and reconcile
later. For playlists, refusing is the better trade — the client already holds an
operation log and queues edits until its home region returns, which turns a
failover into a delay rather than a conflict.

**The ladder.** Written in advance, tested, and given to whoever is on call:

```text
1  keep audio playing            client cache, CDN, licences already issued
2  serve the last home screen    stale precomputed shelves rather than none
3  drop personalisation          editorial and popularity shelves per market
4  queue playlist writes         client outbox, replay when the region returns
5  search from a cached index    top queries answered, long tail degraded
6  pause two log consumers       analytics and personalisation catch up later
```

Read it downward: every step gives up something a listener minds less than the
step above it. Step 6 is where the one-log design shows: nothing can be shed at
ingestion without shedding the royalty consumer's input too, so the analytics and
personalisation consumers are paused instead and the seven-day retention replays
their events when they restart. Operational metrics stay up — they are what tells
you the outage is over.

**Knowing it is broken.** Alert on symptoms rather than causes — play-start
failure rate, time to first audio byte at p99, consumer lag on the log — because
those are what a listener experiences and they stay meaningful when the cause is
something nobody predicted; [Observability, Failure Handling and DR](/learn/sd-observability-and-recovery/)
covers the practice. One number deserves attention: the home screen fans out to
five services, so if each meets its p99 independently all five are fast on only
0.99^5 = 0.95 of requests — about one home-screen request in twenty lands in a
slow tail, not one in a hundred. That is the arithmetic
[Latency and Throughput](/learn/sd-latency-and-throughput/) runs for twenty
calls, and Dean and Barroso's _The Tail at Scale_ (CACM, 2013), publicly
documented, takes it to a hundred-way fan-out that reaches most requests. It is
why fan-out endpoints need deadlines and per-dependency fallbacks rather than
only good averages.

**Chaos engineering** — deliberately injecting failures into production — is a
publicly documented practice from Netflix's own writing, and the previous case
study works through it. One precondition bears repeating: without alerting that
catches the injected failure and a rollback that ends the experiment, an injected
failure is an outage you scheduled.

## Common pitfalls

- **A separate pipeline per consumer.** Three clients emitting three copies of
  the truth, disagreeing about a day's plays, and needing three outages debugged.
- **Exactly-once delivery as a requirement.** It is not available end to end
  across a network; at-least-once delivery plus a deduplicating consumer is, and
  it is what "exactly once" means everywhere it is claimed.
- **Degrading by timeout.** With no ranked ladder, the feature that fails first is
  the one whose dependency happened to be slowest, which is unlikely to be the
  one listeners mind least.
- **Retries with no ceiling.** Every client helping at once is what converts a
  slow dependency into a dead one.
- **Untested failover.** A standby region nobody has served traffic from is a
  hypothesis, and an outage is the wrong moment to test it.

## Interview notes

Two moves carry most of the credit here. **Say what the system does when a
dependency is slow rather than down**, because slow is the common case and where
naive designs fail worst — a fast failure with a fallback beats a timeout holding
a connection for thirty seconds. And **rank the degradation out loud**: naming
the features you would give up, in order, shows you know what the product is for,
which is the part of system design a diagram cannot teach.

## Key takeaways

- **One durable log, many consumers** with their own offsets: the strict
  guarantee lives in the strict consumer, not in the transport.
- At-least-once delivery plus **deduplication on a client-generated event id** is
  how a royalty ledger stays correct without an impossible delivery promise.
- Bursts are absorbed by the **log on the write side and caches on the read
  side**, with client jitter to stop synchronised refreshes.
- Regional failure is answered per data type: **immutable data everywhere,
  personal state with a home region** and a client outbox for its writes.
- The **degradation ladder is written before the outage**, and audio that is
  already playing sits at the top of it.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A proposal replaces the shared log with three pipelines — one for royalties, one for personalisation, one for analytics — so each can be tuned to its own guarantee. Name what that costs.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Three times the client work and three chances to emit a different set of
  events, so the pipelines drift and a discrepancy between the royalty total and
  the analytics dashboard becomes a recurring investigation with no
  authoritative answer. It also triples the operational surface — three
  ingestion paths, three sets of alerts, three backfills — for a benefit the
  single log already provides, since independent consumer offsets let each
  consumer run at its own pace and with its own error handling. The strict
  guarantee belongs in the strict consumer: dedupe and replay in the royalty
  consumer, best effort in personalisation. One record of what happened, several
  readings of it.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. A major album releases at midnight and traffic to its tracks rises roughly tenfold for a few minutes. Which parts of the platform have to be scaled for that, and which absorb it as designed?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Mostly it absorbs. The audio is immutable and the album is a small set of very
  hot objects, so a delivery network serves nearly all of it from cache after
  the first requests, and coalescing those first misses keeps the origin quiet.
  Catalogue reads are cache hits for the same reason — one hot key, already
  resident. The event log takes the extra appends without complaint, and its
  consumers are allowed to lag by minutes, so nothing downstream needs headroom.
  What does need it is the synchronous authorisation path, because every play is
  a fresh signature and ten times the plays is ten times the requests: that
  service is stateless, so it scales horizontally, which is the reason for
  keeping it free of durable state in the first place.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A region holding some listeners' personal state is lost. Audio and catalogue are replicated everywhere. What does a listener in that region experience, and what did the playlist design contribute?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Playback keeps working, because the parts it needs — catalogue metadata, audio
  objects and the stateless authorisation service — are replicated or immutable
  and are served from another region. What breaks is personal state: playlists
  read from a replica and are stale by whatever the replication lag was, and
  writes are refused rather than accepted somewhere that would later have to be
  reconciled. The playlist design makes that refusal survivable: edits are
  operations with client-generated ids held in a local outbox, so the client
  queues them, keeps showing them locally through its own read-your-writes view,
  and replays them when the home region returns. Idempotency means a replayed
  operation that did land is not applied twice.
</PracticeCheck>
