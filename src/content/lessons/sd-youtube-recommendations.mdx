## What you'll learn

- Derive from arithmetic why a billion-item corpus forces a two-stage recommender.
- State what the 2016 RecSys paper documents, and where the design goes beyond it.
- Handle a corpus in which most items have almost no engagement history.
- Name the failure modes of a closed feedback loop and the mechanisms that limit them.

## Intuition

The two-stage funnel — cheap candidate generation, expensive ranking — is
introduced in [the Spotify-like pipeline](/learn/sd-spotify-recommendations/) and
the shape is the same here. Two things about this corpus change what the stages
have to do.

**Most of the catalogue is cold, permanently.** With a billion videos and three
million more each day, the overwhelming majority have almost no engagement
history, and many never will. A recommender ranking on accumulated signal can
therefore only surface what is already popular, so cold start is the normal case
rather than an edge case. Discovery is not a nice property here; it is the
difference between a catalogue and a chart.

**And the loop is closed.** Recommendations produce impressions, impressions
produce watch time, watch time becomes the training data for the next model. The
system learns from a distribution it created: anything it fails to show once
becomes harder to learn about, and anything it over-shows becomes
self-justifying. Every mechanism in the second half of this lesson exists because
of that sentence.

## How it works

**1. Why there are two stages at all.** The arithmetic is worth doing, because it
is the argument.

```text
Why one stage is impossible (teaching assumptions)
  1,000,000,000 videos in the catalogue
  a ranking model costs ~100 µs per item
  1,000,000,000 × 100 µs   =  100,000 s of compute for one feed
  budget for one feed      =       0.1 s
  short by a factor of                1,000,000
```

So the first stage cannot evaluate a model per item. It has to be lookups.

**2. What the paper documents.** The two-stage architecture is _publicly
documented_ in _Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations_ (Covington,
Adams and Sargin, RecSys 2016). Candidate generation reduces what the paper calls
a corpus of millions of videos to hundreds of candidates; the billion-video
catalogue this module assumes makes the funnel steeper than the paper's. The
stage is framed as a classification problem — given a user's history, predict the
video watched next — and at serving time it is a **nearest-neighbour search in an
embedding space** rather than a model run per item, which is exactly how a lookup
replaces a scoring pass. Ranking then scores those hundreds with a richer model
whose features include embeddings of watch history, the language of viewer and
video, the time since the last watch, and how many times this user has already
been shown it. Video age enters the paper elsewhere, as an _example age_ feature
in candidate generation — the age of the training example, not a ranking input.
The paper's ranking objective is **expected watch time per impression rather than
click-through**, on the stated reasoning that ranking by click-through promotes
deceptive videos the viewer does not complete — "clickbait" — whereas watch time
better captures _engagement_. Engagement is the paper's word: it does not claim
to measure satisfaction, and says elsewhere that ground truth for satisfaction is
rarely available.

**3. Candidate sources in practice.** Beyond the learned nearest-neighbour
source, _generally accepted industry architecture_ is to union several cheap
retrievers and let ranking sort out the result:

| Source                  | How it works                                    | Answers                    |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Embedding neighbours    | Nearest videos to the viewer's history vector   | Personalised similarity    |
| Subscriptions           | Recent uploads from channels the viewer follows | The explicit relationship  |
| Watch-next continuation | Videos co-watched after the one playing now     | The next-video panel       |
| Topical and semantic    | Neighbours by title, transcript and topic       | Cold videos with no plays  |
| Trending per locale     | Rapid view-rate risers in a region              | Newness and shared context |

The union is deliberately over-inclusive: anything absent from it can never be
recommended, so recall matters here and precision is ranking's problem.

**4. The objective, and its limits.** Predicting watch time fixes the worst
failure of predicting clicks: a click is registered before the viewer knows
whether the title was honest. It introduces its own biases: watch time favours
long videos over short ones that answered the question, and it cannot distinguish
content that satisfies from content that merely holds attention. The usual correction, _generally accepted_ rather than documented for
any one platform, is to blend predicted watch time with signals only a satisfied
viewer produces — likes, survey responses, returns to the channel — and with
negatives such as "not interested" and dismissals.

**5. Cold start as the normal case.** A video with no history is placed by what
is known about it rather than what has happened to it: its channel's record, and
its position in an embedding space derived from title, transcript and topic. It
then needs impressions to learn from, which is what an **exploration budget**
buys — a small share of slots given to items the model is uncertain about rather
than to its top-scoring predictions. Without one the loop closes: nothing new is
shown, so nothing new accumulates signal, so nothing new is ever shown.

<Callout variant="warning">
  **Engagement is a proxy, not the goal.** A metric measuring how long someone
  stayed is a stand-in for whether the time was worth their while, and
  optimising the proxy hard enough parts company with the thing it stood for.
  Treating that as a modelling detail rather than a design constraint is how
  recommenders end up amplifying content that performs well and serves nobody.
  The mechanisms that keep the two aligned — satisfaction signals, diversity
  constraints, quality-layer demotion, and holdout groups measuring the long-run
  effect rather than the session — belong in the architecture, not a later
  clean-up.
</Callout>

**6. The serving path.** Feature generation, model training and the embedding
index are offline work measured in hours; the indexes and per-user vectors sit in
memory. Inside the request: retrieve candidates, rank them, apply policy that is
not the model's business — already watched, region and age restrictions,
dismissed channels, per-channel diversity caps — and return a page. The budget is
milliseconds; everything expensive happened earlier.

## Trade-offs

**Recall against cost at candidate generation.** Widening the union catches more
of the catalogue and costs lookups and ranking work on items that will not
survive. Narrowing it saves both and quietly caps what the system can ever
recommend.

**Personalisation against discovery.** A model fitted tightly to a viewer's
history predicts their next watch well and narrows what they are ever offered.
Exploration and diversity constraints spend some predicted engagement to keep the
corpus reachable.

**Offline precompute against freshness.** Batch scoring is what makes serving
affordable and it is hours old. A video uploaded an hour ago exists only in the
streaming path, so a fresh candidate source has to run beside the precomputed
one.

## Common pitfalls

- **Scoring the catalogue per request.** It is the obvious design and the
  arithmetic above says it is a million times too slow.
- **Optimising clicks.** Abundant, cheap, and it rewards the title rather than
  the video.
- **Treating cold start as an exception.** On a user-upload catalogue it is the
  majority state, and a design that handles it in a special case is handling the
  main case in a special case.
- **Judging a ranking change offline only.** Offline metrics measure agreement
  with behaviour the previous ranking produced, so they favour the incumbent;
  only a live experiment measures the change.
- **No exploration budget.** Nothing appears to break, and the corpus silently
  shrinks to whatever the model already knew about.

## Interview notes

Lead with the arithmetic, not the architecture. "A billion items at a hundred
microseconds each is a hundred thousand seconds of compute for a page with a
hundred-millisecond budget, so the first stage has to be lookups rather than a
model" earns the two-stage design instead of asserting it.

Then name the objective and say why it is not clicks, and be ready for the
follow-up: what watch time gets wrong. A candidate who can state a weakness of
the metric they have recommended is describing a system they have thought about
operating.

## Key takeaways

- Two stages are **forced by arithmetic**: candidate generation must cut a
  billion items to hundreds using lookups, not model evaluations.
- The 2016 RecSys paper documents candidate generation as a classification
  problem served by **nearest-neighbour search in an embedding space**, and a
  ranking model that predicts **expected watch time rather than clicks**.
- On a user-upload catalogue **most items are cold**, so content-derived
  placement and an exploration budget are core mechanisms rather than fallbacks.
- The **feedback loop is closed**: the model trains on impressions it chose, so
  concentration and narrowing are the default outcome without deliberate
  counterweights.
- Engagement metrics are **proxies for satisfaction**, and keeping them aligned
  is an architectural responsibility.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. A colleague proposes dropping candidate generation and ranking the whole catalogue directly with the good model, arguing that hardware is cheap and the model is what produces quality.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  Do the arithmetic in front of them. A billion items at roughly a hundred
  microseconds of model evaluation each is about a hundred thousand seconds of
  compute to build one feed, against a request budget of about a tenth of a
  second — six orders of magnitude apart. Hardware does not close a gap that
  size: even parallelised perfectly across a thousand cores it is still a
  hundred seconds for a single user's page, and there are tens of thousands of
  pages per second. The deeper point is that candidate generation is not a
  degraded version of ranking; it is a different kind of operation. It answers
  with lookups against precomputed structures — nearest neighbours in an
  embedding space, subscription lists, co-watch tables — whose cost is
  independent of catalogue size. That is what makes the funnel work: the stage
  that must touch everything is cheap per item, and the stage that is expensive
  per item touches only hundreds.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. The team reports that videos uploaded in the last week are almost never recommended, even to viewers subscribed to the channel. What is going on, and what fixes it?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Two causes, and they compound. First, ranking features are dominated by
  accumulated engagement, and a new video has none — so it scores below older
  videos with a history, no matter how good it is. Second, the loop closes
  around that: with no impressions it accumulates no signal, so it never scores
  higher, and a model retrained on those logs learns that new videos do not
  perform. The fixes address both halves. Give the model recency as an explicit
  input so it can learn how engagement behaves as a function of age instead of
  inferring that new means bad. Place cold videos by what is known rather than
  what has happened — the channel's record, and embeddings derived from title,
  transcript and topic. Then check what becomes of the subscription source's
  candidates: this design already retrieves recent uploads from followed
  channels, so a subscriber who never sees the video is being dropped by ranking
  rather than missed by retrieval, and the explicit relationship has to survive
  scoring. And reserve an exploration budget of slots for uncertain items, which
  is the only mechanism that generates the missing data rather than working
  around it.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. Watch time is now the ranking objective, and the paper's reasoning is that click-through promotes clickbait while watch time better captures engagement. Where does watch time still go wrong as a stand-in for satisfaction, and what would you add?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Accept the premise — watch time genuinely is the better of the two, because a
  click is recorded before the viewer knows anything about the video and watch
  time is not. It is still a proxy, and it fails in three recognisable ways. It
  has a length bias: a twenty-minute video that half satisfies beats a
  three-minute one that answers the question completely. It cannot distinguish
  satisfaction from compulsion, so content that holds attention without being
  worth the time scores identically to content that was. And it says nothing
  about what happens afterwards — whether the viewer came back, or regretted the
  evening. The additions follow from each: normalise against video length or
  predict completion as well as duration; blend in signals only a satisfied
  viewer produces, such as likes, surveys and returns to the channel, and
  subtract the explicit negatives like dismissals and "not interested"; and
  measure with long-run holdout groups rather than session metrics, because the
  failure mode being guarded against is precisely one that looks like success
  inside a single session.
</PracticeCheck>
