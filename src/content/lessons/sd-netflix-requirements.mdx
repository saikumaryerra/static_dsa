## What you'll learn

- Separate what a Netflix-like platform does from how well it has to do it.
- Estimate concurrent streams, egress, catalogue storage and playback-state writes from stated assumptions.
- Name the number that decides the architecture, and say what it decides.
- Scope the design out loud, including the features this module refuses.

## Intuition

**A streaming platform is two systems sharing a login.** Almost every decision in
the next five lessons falls out of that split, so it is worth naming before any
box is drawn.

The **control plane** is JSON. Who are you, which profile is watching, what rows
belong on the home page, where did this profile stop, may this device play this
title. Payloads are kilobytes, the unit is QPS — queries per second — and the
thing readers feel is p99 latency.

The **data plane** is video bytes. Its unit is terabits per second, its payloads
are hours long, and no application server ever touches it. The two planes have
different scaling laws, different failure modes and different owners, and
conflating them is the mistake that makes streaming look impossible.

The second idea is that **the catalogue is small**. Tens of thousands of titles
is the shape of a curated, licensed catalogue — a teaching assumption here, and
the one the comparative module contrasts against a user-upload platform. A small
catalogue means the metadata fits in memory, next week's popularity is
predictable, and every title can be encoded slowly and carefully exactly once.
Nothing else about this system is small.

## How it works

**Functional requirements**, stated as capabilities rather than screens:

| Capability                 | What it has to mean                                                    |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accounts and profiles      | One paying account, up to five profiles, each with its own taste       |
| Catalogue and metadata     | Titles, seasons, episodes, artwork, availability by region             |
| Search and discovery       | Title and person search, plus a personalised home page of ranked rows  |
| Playback                   | Start a title on any device, seek, pause, resume, adapt to the network |
| Subtitles and audio tracks | Several subtitle languages and several audio tracks per title          |
| Watch history              | An append-only record of what a profile watched                        |
| Continue watching          | The current position per profile per title, shared across devices      |
| Multiple devices           | Phone, browser, console and television, with different codec support   |

**Non-functional requirements** are where the architecture comes from. Playback
start is the availability target that matters: a home page that renders a stale
row is a poor evening, a title that will not start is a cancelled subscription.
Set start success at four nines and time-to-first-frame at a p99 of two seconds,
and treat discovery as one nine less. Consistency is per profile rather than
global — a profile must see its own last position (read-your-writes), while two
profiles never need to agree on anything. History is durable; recommendations are
rebuildable, so they are a cache with a long fill time rather than a database of
record.

**Out of scope**, said out loud: live events, user uploads, downloads for offline
viewing, billing and payments, and the rights-management workflow that decides
which title is available where. Each is a system in its own right, and the last
two are where a real platform spends organisational effort rather than engineering
scale.

**The estimate.** Assumptions first, arithmetic visible, results to one
significant figure.

```text
Assumptions (teaching assumptions, all challengeable)
  200,000,000 subscriber accounts, up to 5 profiles each
  100,000,000 profiles watch on a given day
  2 hours of viewing per watching profile
  4 Mbps average delivered bitrate across the rendition ladder
  a day rounds to 100,000 s

Concurrent streams and egress
  100,000,000 × 7,200 s      =  720,000,000,000 watch-seconds/day
  720,000,000,000 ÷ 100,000  =        7,200,000 average concurrent streams
  7,200,000 × 4 Mbps         =   28,800,000 Mbps  ≈  30 Tbps average
  peak ≈ 3 × average                              ≈  90 Tbps peak
```

```text
Catalogue storage
  50,000 titles averaging 5,000 s of content each
  ~50 Mbps stored per title-second across resolutions, codecs and audio
  50,000 × 5,000 s           =      250,000,000 title-seconds
  250,000,000 × 50 Mbit      =   12,500,000,000 Mbit  ≈  1.6 PB
  plus subtitles and artwork                          ≈  2 PB of renditions

  mezzanine masters at ~100 Mbps, kept but never delivered
  250,000,000 × 100 Mbit     =   25,000,000,000 Mbit  ≈  3 PB in archive

Control-plane rates
  100,000,000 × 3 app opens  =      300,000,000 home builds/day
  ÷ 100,000                  =    3,000 /s average,  ≈  9,000 /s peak
  100,000,000 × 4 titles     =      400,000,000 playback starts/day
  ÷ 100,000                  =    4,000 /s average,  ≈ 12,000 /s peak

Playback-state writes
  7,200,000 streams, one position update every 30 s
  7,200,000 ÷ 30             =  240,000 writes/s, sustained
```

Now read them, because an estimate that ends in a figure has not finished.

**Ninety terabits per second at peak decides the architecture.** At 100 Gbps per
interface that is nine hundred saturated links, which no origin fleet owns; the
bytes have to be sitting near viewers before they are asked for. Everything in
the video-delivery lesson is a consequence of this one line.

**Two petabytes is the surprise.** Every rendition the platform will ever deliver
would fit in a single rack of dense storage, and it grows only when the catalogue
does. The mezzanine masters are a few petabytes more, but they sit in archive
storage and are read by encoders rather than by viewers, so they cost nothing at
delivery time. Compare the
video platform estimated in [back-of-envelope estimation](/learn/sd-capacity-estimation/),
where 10 million viewers at 30 minutes each gave 180,000 concurrent streams,
540 Gbps average and roughly 2 Tbps peak, against 100 TB/day and about 36 PB/year
of storage. This platform is roughly fifty times larger on egress while its
storage is effectively flat, because everyone watches far more and nobody
uploads.

**Three thousand home builds per second is an ordinary web service.** A cached,
precomputed home page at that rate is a small fleet — which is why personalisation
can afford to be expensive offline.

<Callout variant="note">
  **The 240,000 writes per second is the one people miss.** Continue watching
  looks like a small feature next to video delivery, yet its write rate is
  eighty times the home-page rate, because it is generated by streams rather
  than by people tapping. It is a data-plane-shaped load hiding inside the
  control plane, and it gets [its own
  lesson](/learn/sd-netflix-playback-state/).
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Profiles as a first-class entity.** Keying personalisation, history and
position to a profile rather than an account costs an extra identifier in every
request, every cache key and every table. It buys recommendations that are not an
average of a household, which is most of the product's value.

**A curated catalogue.** Licensing and ingest become an organisation rather than
an API, and the catalogue can never grow the way a user-upload platform's does.
In exchange, popularity is predictable, encoding cost is bounded and paid once,
and the whole catalogue can be pushed to caches before anyone requests it.

**Playback over freshness.** Choosing playback start as the strictest target
means accepting that a title added an hour ago may be missing from some home
pages, and that a row can be built from yesterday's model. Both are deliberate.

## Common pitfalls

- **Sizing for the average.** Thirty terabits per second is comfortable and
  irrelevant; the system exists at ninety. Read every rate in this module as an
  average unless it is labelled a peak, and size for the peak — about three times
  the average on these assumptions.
- **Treating watch history as one thing.** The mutable resume position and the
  append-only history have different write rates, different durability needs and
  different consistency requirements, and merging them into one table is what
  makes the write path unaffordable.
- **Designing the catalogue store for a billion rows.** Partitioning fifty
  thousand titles is cost with no benefit, and it forfeits the option of holding
  the whole catalogue in memory.
- **Forgetting that subtitles and audio tracks multiply.** Ten audio tracks and
  twenty subtitle languages per title change the manifest, the ladder and the
  storage figure — the tempting error is to price only the video.
- **Quoting a bitrate without saying which one.** Four megabits per second
  delivered and fifty megabits per second stored are both true here and differ by
  a factor of twelve.

## Interview notes

Say the two planes in the first two minutes. "The API tier is a few thousand
user-initiated requests per second, which is unremarkable; the delivery tier is
tens of terabits per second, which is the whole problem" reframes the question before
anyone starts drawing, and it is the sentence that separates a candidate who has
thought about streaming from one reciting a component list.

Then say the catalogue is small, and say what that buys. Interviewers ask about a
streaming platform partly to see whether the candidate notices that its catalogue
is nothing like a video-sharing site's, because the two need different ingest,
different storage tiers and different recommendation architectures.

## Key takeaways

- A Netflix-like platform is a **small control plane** measured in QPS and a
  **huge data plane** measured in terabits per second; the two scale separately.
- On stated assumptions this design needs roughly **7.2 million concurrent
  streams, 30 Tbps average and 90 Tbps peak** — the number that forces content
  delivery near viewers.
- The deliverable catalogue is about **2 PB of renditions and static**, so storage
  is not the problem here, unlike a platform whose users upload.
- **Playback start is the availability target**; discovery is allowed to be one
  nine less and slightly stale.
- Continue watching generates about **240,000 writes per second sustained**, a
  data-plane rate inside a control-plane feature.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. The catalogue holds 50,000 titles rather than 500 million. Name two design decisions that changes.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  First, catalogue metadata becomes a cacheable, in-memory data set rather than
  a partitioned store: fifty thousand titles with artwork references and
  availability rules is a few hundred megabytes, so every service can hold a
  copy and a metadata read never touches a database. Second, recommendation
  serving can score a large fraction of the catalogue per profile instead of
  needing a separate candidate-generation stage to cut billions of items down to
  hundreds. A third follows for free: with a small catalogue, popularity is
  predictable enough to push copies of every title to caches before anyone asks
  for one.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. The estimate gives 3,000 home builds per second and 240,000 position writes per second. Why is the second number the one that changes the design?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Because they are generated by different things. Home builds are generated by
  people opening the app, so the rate tracks user actions and stays in the range
  a normal cached web tier serves. Position writes are generated by streams that
  are already running, so the rate tracks concurrent playback and continues at
  full volume for hours with nobody touching a device. At 240,000 writes per
  second a single relational primary is out of the question — the reference
  ceiling is a few thousand indexed writes per second — so this feature needs a
  write-optimised store, a lower update frequency, or both. The read-shaped
  feature is affordable; the write-shaped one is the design problem.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. Someone proposes adding user uploads to the scope, arguing that the platform already transcodes video so the pipeline exists.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  The transcoding step is the only part that carries over, and it is not the
  expensive part of the change. Uploads make the catalogue unbounded, so storage
  moves from a fixed 2 PB to a figure that grows every day and needs tiering.
  They make popularity unpredictable, which removes the ability to preposition
  content in caches and turns delivery into a long-tail problem. They add
  moderation, copyright matching and abuse handling, which are organisationally
  larger than anything in this design. And they invert the encode economics:
  encoding carefully once per title is affordable for fifty thousand curated
  titles and not for continuous user upload, where publish latency competes with
  quality. That is a different system, which the YouTube-like module designs
  separately.
</PracticeCheck>
