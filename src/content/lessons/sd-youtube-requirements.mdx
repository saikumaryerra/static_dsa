## What you'll learn

- Say what changes when the catalogue is uploaded by users rather than licensed.
- Estimate egress, ingest, storage growth, transcoding compute and view rate from stated assumptions.
- Name the two numbers that decide this architecture, and what each one decides.
- Scope the design out loud, including what this module refuses to build.

## Intuition

**A video-sharing platform is a streaming platform that has lost control of its
catalogue.** Almost everything the next six lessons do follows from that one
difference, so it is worth naming before a box is drawn.

The [Netflix-like design](/learn/sd-netflix-requirements/) starts from tens of
thousands of licensed titles. Popularity is predictable a week ahead, every title
is worth encoding slowly and carefully, and the whole catalogue fits in a rack.
Here anyone uploads, nobody approves in advance, the catalogue is of the order of
a billion videos, and it grows by millions every day. The audience is larger too,
but that is a quantitative change. **An unbounded catalogue is a qualitative
one**: it removes the ability to know in advance what to cache, what to encode
well, and what is safe to publish.

The second model carries through the module. **The head and the tail are two
different systems sharing a name.** Take as a teaching assumption that
the most-watched 0.1% of videos collect around 90% of the views. Both halves of
that sentence are load-bearing. The head is a delivery problem: hot, spiky,
intensely cacheable, and capable of a hundredfold surge in an hour. The tail is a
storage problem: cold, enormous, watched a handful of times ever, and stored
regardless. A design that optimises for one and forgets the other either stalls
on a video that goes viral or goes broke keeping videos nobody watches.

The third: **trust work sits in the publish path**. Because uploads are not
curated, moderation, copyright matching and abuse handling are not features
bolted on later — they are steps between "the bytes arrived" and "anyone can
watch this".

## How it works

**Functional requirements**, stated as capabilities rather than screens:

| Capability                 | What it has to mean                                                          |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Upload                     | Any signed-in user sends a file of any size over any network, and loses none |
| Processing                 | Transcode to a ladder, make thumbnails, check for abuse and copyright        |
| Playback                   | Start any video on any device, seek, adapt to the network                    |
| Channels and subscriptions | Every uploader is a channel; a viewer subscribes and receives its new videos |
| Search                     | Find a video by title, description, transcript or channel, plus autocomplete |
| Recommendations            | A home feed and a next-video panel drawn from the whole catalogue            |
| Engagement                 | Comments, likes and view counts, all moderated                               |
| Notifications              | Tell subscribers, and only interested ones, that a channel published         |
| Creator analytics          | Per-video views, watch time and audience breakdown, for the uploader         |

**Non-functional requirements** are where the architecture comes from, and the
strictest one here is not availability.

**Durability of an accepted upload comes first.** Once the platform has said
"received", that file is often the only copy in the world — the phone that
recorded it may be full, wiped or lost tomorrow. Playback start success is next,
at four nines, with time to first frame at a p99 of about one second, because
sessions here are minutes rather than hours and a slow start is a larger fraction
of the experience. Publish latency is a stated target of minutes, and it competes
directly with encoding quality. Counters — views and likes — are eventually
consistent and may lag by seconds; comment ordering inside one thread must be
stable. Search must find a new upload within minutes. Upload availability is
allowed a nine less than playback: a failed upload is retried by the client, a
failed playback is a viewer who left.

**Out of scope**, said out loud: advertising and monetisation, creator payouts,
rights negotiation, and the client applications themselves. Live streaming is
deferred to the [playback lesson](/learn/sd-youtube-playback/) and treated at
concept level there, because it breaks assumptions the rest of the module relies
on.

**The estimate.** Assumptions first, arithmetic visible, results to one
significant figure.

```text
Assumptions (teaching assumptions, all challengeable)
  2,000,000,000 registered viewers, 500,000,000 watching on a given day
  40 minutes of viewing per watching viewer, 8 videos started each
  a rendition ladder of six rungs, ≈ 12 Mbps stored per video-second
  3 Mbps average delivered bitrate across that ladder
  500,000 hours of new video per day, averaging 10 minutes per upload
  a catalogue of 1,000,000,000 videos
  a day rounds to 100,000 s

Egress
  500,000,000 × 2,400 s        =  1,200,000,000,000 watch-seconds/day
  1,200,000,000,000 ÷ 100,000  =         12,000,000 average concurrent streams
  12,000,000 × 3 Mbps          =         36,000,000 Mbps  ≈   36 Tbps average
  peak ≈ 3 × average                                      ≈  100 Tbps peak
```

```text
Ingest and transcoding
  500,000 h × 3,600 s          =  1,800,000,000 s of source video/day
  1,800,000,000 ÷ 600 s        =      3,000,000 uploads/day
  3,000,000 ÷ 100,000          =             30 uploads/s average, ≈ 90/s peak
  1,800,000,000 ÷ 100,000      =         18,000 s of video arriving every second
  a ladder costs ≈ 10 core-s per source-second
  18,000 × 10                  =        180,000 cores, continuously

Stored bytes
  the six-rung ladder ≈ 12 Mbps stored, so a 10-minute video ≈ 1 GB
  1,800,000,000 × 12 Mbit      = 21,600,000,000 Mbit  ≈  3 PB/day
  × 365                                              ≈  1 EB/year
  1,000,000,000 videos × 1 GB                        ≈  1 EB of catalogue

Control plane
  500,000,000 × 8 videos       =  4,000,000,000 video starts/day
  ÷ 100,000                    =         40,000 starts/s average, ≈ 120,000/s peak
```

Now read them, because an estimate that ends in a figure has not finished.

**A hundred terabits per second at peak** says what it says for any video
platform: no origin fleet serves that, and the bytes must sit near viewers. What
differs is that popularity is unknown, so caches cannot be filled in advance —
what the [playback lesson](/learn/sd-youtube-playback/) is built around.

**Three petabytes a day is the number with no counterpart** in a curated
catalogue. Egress is a rate that plateaus when the audience does; storage is the
running total of every upload ever accepted, and it climbs on flat traffic
forever. Notice that a billion videos is about a year of uploads at three million
a day: these round assumptions describe a platform whose upload rate has grown,
so recent years dominate the catalogue. An exabyte of catalogue against a hot
working set of roughly a petabyte — the top million videos at about a gigabyte
each — is one part in a thousand doing 90% of the work.

**Eighteen thousand seconds of video arriving every second** costs 180,000 cores
on the stated assumption. That is a fleet, and it is why transcoding is queued
work rather than part of the request.

<Callout variant="note">
  **Compare the two case studies on one axis.** A Netflix-like platform stores
  about 2 PB and it stays there; this one adds about 3 PB every day. Same
  business — video to a screen — and the ingest model alone moves storage by
  three orders of magnitude, from a fixed cost to an integral.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

**Publish latency against encode quality.** Encoding carefully takes longer, and
a creator watching a progress bar counts minutes. The generally accepted
resolution is to publish something watchable quickly and improve it later for the
videos that earn the attention.

**Unbounded catalogue against predictable cost.** Accepting every upload is the
product, and the cost is that storage is decided by users rather than by a budget
— which forces tiering, retention and lazy encoding as design work rather than
housekeeping. The [scale lesson](/learn/sd-youtube-scale/) takes the three cost
centres apart.

**Counters that are cheap against counters that are exact.** Making the view
count strongly consistent at 120,000 increments per second at peak would dominate
the design. Allowing it to be seconds behind costs a number that is briefly wrong
and buys a write path that fits.

## Common pitfalls

- **Sizing the catalogue from today's uploads.** Three million uploads a day is a
  rate; the bill is its integral over the platform's life, and the tempting error
  is to quote the daily figure as the total.
- **Treating this as a Netflix-like design with more titles.** The distinguishing
  facts are unpredictable popularity and unbounded storage, neither of which is a
  matter of scale.
- **Leaving moderation until after the architecture is drawn.** It is a step in
  the publish path with a latency budget, so adding it late means re-cutting the
  pipeline.
- **Quoting one bitrate.** Three megabits per second delivered and twelve
  megabits per second stored are both correct here and differ by a factor of
  four.
- **Forgetting the creator.** Analytics, upload status and takedown appeals are a
  second product with its own read patterns, invisible if you design only the
  viewer's screen.

## Interview notes

Open by contrasting the ingest model, not the size. "The audience is bigger than
a subscription streamer's, but the thing that changes the architecture is that
the catalogue is user-generated: popularity is unpredictable, so nothing can be
prepositioned, and storage grows without bound" is a sentence that reframes the
question in fifteen seconds.

Then give two numbers rather than five. Peak egress decides delivery; daily
storage growth decides tiering and encoding strategy. The rest is supporting
arithmetic, and reciting it costs the time you need for the pipeline.

## Key takeaways

- The defining difference from a curated streaming platform is **an unbounded,
  unpredictable catalogue**, not a bigger audience.
- On the stated assumptions: **36 Tbps average and about 100 Tbps peak** of
  egress, **3 PB per day** of new storage, **180,000 cores** of transcoding, and
  **40,000 video starts per second**.
- **Egress plateaus; storage accumulates.** The daily growth figure, not the
  total, decides tiering and retention.
- The head and the tail are different systems: roughly **a petabyte of hot
  content inside an exabyte of catalogue** serves most views.
- **Durability of an accepted upload is the strictest promise** here, because the
  platform often holds the only copy.

## Practice

Work each one out before opening the answer.

**1. The audience is five times a subscription streamer's and the catalogue is roughly twenty thousand times larger. Which of those two changes the architecture more, and why?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={1} total={3}>
  The catalogue, and it is not close. A five-times-larger audience multiplies
  numbers that already had a known shape: more edge capacity, more application
  servers, more cache. Nothing about the design has to be rethought. A catalogue
  that is twenty thousand times larger and produced by users removes three
  properties the streaming design depended on. Popularity is no longer
  predictable, so content cannot be pushed to caches before it is requested.
  Storage is no longer fixed, so it becomes a running total that grows every day
  and needs tiering and retention. And encoding can no longer be slow and
  careful for everything, because the cost is paid per upload rather than per
  licensed title. Scale multiplies a design; an unbounded catalogue replaces
  parts of it.
</PracticeCheck>

**2. The estimate gives 100 Tbps at peak and 3 PB per day of new storage. Which of the two can be substantially reduced by policy alone — a rule about what the platform keeps, with no viewer noticing and nothing new to build?**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={2} total={3}>
  Storage. Egress is set by what people watch and at what bitrate, so no rule
  the platform writes down moves it: fewer views and a lower delivered quality
  are both visible, and the one lever that is not — a newer codec carrying the
  same picture in fewer bits — is engineering paid for in encode compute rather
  than a policy anyone can adopt on Monday. Storage growth is set by what is
  uploaded and by how many renditions are kept, and the second half is entirely
  the platform's choice. Generating the full ladder only for videos that attract
  views, and keeping only a low rendition for the rest, reduces the daily growth
  substantially without any viewer noticing, because the videos affected are the
  ones almost nobody opens. One number is a property of the audience; the other
  is a rule someone chose.
</PracticeCheck>

**3. A reviewer says durability of uploads cannot be the strictest requirement, because playback is what viewers judge the product by. Answer them.**

<PracticeCheck slug={frontmatter.slug} index={3} total={3}>
  Both matter, but they fail differently and that is what ranks them. A playback
  failure is a viewer who retries, reloads, or comes back later; the video is
  still there and the loss is one session. A lost upload is unrecoverable — the
  platform accepted the file and the creator's copy may be gone, so no retry
  exists at any layer. Ranking durability first does not mean neglecting
  playback; it means the two get different mechanisms. Durability is bought with
  replication across failure domains and with acknowledging the upload only once
  the bytes are committed to storage that survives losing a machine or a region.
  Playback availability is bought with caches, replicas and degradation. The
  requirement that is strictest is the one whose failure cannot be undone.
</PracticeCheck>
